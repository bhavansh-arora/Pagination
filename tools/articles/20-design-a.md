---
Most small business websites convert between 1 and 3% of visitors into enquiries. The best convert 5 to 10%. Same traffic, three times the leads. The difference isn't magic. It's a series of small, sensible decisions that make it easier for a visitor to say yes.

## First, measure

You can't improve what you don't track.

- Set up analytics with conversion events: form submissions, phone clicks, booking completions, WhatsApp clicks
- Work out your conversion rate: enquiries ÷ visitors
- Note which pages people land on and leave from

## 1. Clarify your headline

Within five seconds, a visitor should know what you do, who it's for and where. Test yours on someone who doesn't know your business.

Weak: "Welcome to Harbour & Co."
Strong: "Mortgage advice Basingstoke trusts, from people who pick up the phone."

## 2. One primary call to action

Decide the single most valuable action (book a call, get a quote, request a preview) and repeat it consistently: header, hero, after each major section, footer. See [call-to-action examples](/blog/call-to-action-examples/).

## 3. Put proof where decisions happen

- Review rating and count near the top
- Testimonials next to calls to action
- Logos of clients, accreditations and press
- Numbers: years, customers, projects

More in [20 website trust signals](/blog/website-trust-signals/).

## 4. Reduce form friction

Every field costs you completions.

- Ask only what you need to start the conversation
- Use multi-step forms for longer ones
- Make the button specific: "Get my free quote"
- Say what happens next and how fast

See [contact forms that convert](/blog/contact-form-best-practices/).

## 5. Offer more than one way to get in touch

Some people call. Some prefer a form. Many would rather message. Offer phone, form, WhatsApp and booking, and let the visitor choose. See [adding WhatsApp to your website](/blog/whatsapp-for-business-website/).

## 6. Speed up

Each extra second of load time loses visitors, especially on mobile. Compress images, remove unneeded scripts and test on a real phone. See [Core Web Vitals explained](/blog/core-web-vitals-explained/).

## 7. Design for thumbs

Most visitors are on phones.

- Tappable phone number
- Large buttons
- Sticky call-to-action bar
- Readable text without zooming

See [mobile-first web design](/blog/mobile-first-design/).

## 8. Answer objections

List the reasons people hesitate (price, trust, time, risk) and address each on the page: pricing guidance, guarantees, process, FAQs.

## 9. Lower the risk

- Free consultation or quote
- Money-back guarantee
- No-obligation language
- A preview or sample before commitment

Our own [free website preview](/free-website-preview/) exists for this reason: seeing the work first removes the biggest risk in hiring a designer.

## 10. Use real photos

Photos of real people, premises and work outperform stock. Faces build trust.

## 11. Create focused landing pages

Send ad and campaign traffic to pages built for one offer and one audience. See [landing page vs website](/blog/landing-page-vs-website/).

## 12. Add a secondary conversion

Not everyone is ready to enquire. Offer a smaller step: a guide, a calculator, a newsletter. Then follow up.

## 13. Follow up fast

A lead contacted within minutes is far more likely to convert than one contacted the next day. Conversion doesn't end at the form.

## What to test first

| Change | Effort | Typical impact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rewrite headline | Low | High |
| Add CTA above the fold | Low | High |
| Shorten form | Low | High |
| Add reviews near CTA | Low | Medium to high |
| Speed up page | Medium | Medium to high |
| Add click-to-call / WhatsApp | Low | Medium |
| Add pricing guidance | Low | Medium |

Change one thing at a time where you can, and compare a few weeks before and after.

## Look at real behaviour

Free tools such as Microsoft Clarity record sessions and heatmaps. Watching ten visitors use your site will reveal problems no checklist can.

## When tweaks aren't enough

If the site is slow, dated and structurally confused, optimisation only goes so far. See [12 signs you need a new website](/blog/signs-you-need-a-new-website/).

## Frequently asked questions

### What's a good conversion rate?
For service businesses, 3 to 5% is solid and 5 to 10% is excellent. It varies by industry and traffic source.

### Do pop-ups increase conversions?
They can capture emails but often annoy visitors, especially on mobile. Use sparingly and never to block the main content immediately.

### How much traffic do I need to run tests?
For formal A/B tests, thousands of visitors. For smaller sites, make sensible improvements and compare before and after.

=== homepage-design-best-practices
title: Homepage Design: 14 Best Practices for Small Business Websites
desc: Fourteen homepage design best practices that help small business websites make a strong first impression, communicate clearly and turn visitors into enquiries.

takeaways: A homepage has seconds to say what you do, for whom and what to do next | Lead with one strong image and one clear call to action | Order sections by what the visitor needs to know to trust you | Keep it fast, focused and easy to scan on a phone
---
Your homepage is the busiest page on your site and the one that decides whether visitors stay. It isn't the place to say everything. It's the place to say the right things in the right order.

## 1. A headline that explains

Say what you do and who it's for. If someone reads only one line, it should be enough.

- "Powering Ireland's solar future."
- "Websites that win you clients."

## 2. A supporting sentence

One or two lines adding detail: services, location, what makes you different.

## 3. One primary call to action

A single, obvious button: "Get a free quote", "Book a call", "Check your date". A secondary link (see our work, learn more) can sit beside it.

## 4. A strong visual

One excellent image or short video that shows your work, your team or the result you create. Avoid carousels; most visitors never see past the first slide.

## 5. Proof near the top

A review rating, client logos, awards or a key number ("1,200+ installs"). Trust needs to arrive early.

## 6. What you offer

A brief overview of your services with links to detail pages. Three to six clear items.

## 7. Who it's for

Help visitors self-identify: industries you serve, problems you solve, or separate paths for different audiences.

## 8. How it works

Three or four steps from first contact to result. Removing uncertainty reduces hesitation.

## 9. Evidence

Testimonials, case studies, portfolio pieces. Real names, real photos, real numbers.

## 10. About, briefly

A short introduction with a face. People buy from people.

## 11. Answer objections

An FAQ or a few lines on pricing, guarantees and timing.

## 12. A final call to action

End with a clear invitation and a way to act, ideally a short form or booking widget.

## 13. A useful footer

Contact details, hours, service areas, key links, social profiles and legal pages. Many visitors scroll straight there.

## 14. Speed and mobile

All of the above must load fast and work on a phone. See [mobile-first web design](/blog/mobile-first-design/).

## A proven order

1. Hero: headline, subline, CTA, image
2. Proof strip
3. Services
4. How it works
5. Featured work or case study
6. Testimonials
7. About
8. FAQ
9. Final CTA
10. Footer

Adapt it, but notice the logic: what you do, why trust you, how it works, proof, act.

## Common mistakes

- **Vague headlines:** "Solutions for a better tomorrow"
- **Sliders** hiding key messages
- **Too many competing buttons**
- **Walls of text**
- **Stock photos** of handshakes
- **Auto-playing sound**
- **No visible contact details**
- **Hidden navigation on desktop**

## Design principles

- **Hierarchy:** the most important thing is the biggest
- **White space:** room to breathe makes content easier to absorb
- **Consistency:** a small palette and two typefaces
- **Contrast:** buttons and text must stand out
- **Accessibility:** readable sizes and colour contrast ([basics](/blog/website-accessibility-basics/))

## Words matter as much as design

A beautiful homepage with unclear copy won't convert. See [website copywriting for small businesses](/blog/website-copywriting-guide/).

## Test it

Show your homepage to someone unfamiliar with your business for five seconds. Then ask: what does this company do? Who is it for? What would you click? If they can't answer, simplify.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a homepage be?
Long enough to cover the essentials above. On mobile, people scroll happily if each section earns its place.

### Should I have a slider?
Generally no. A single strong message outperforms rotating ones.

### Should the homepage target a keyword?
Yes, usually your main service plus location, in the title tag and headline.

=== call-to-action-examples
title: Call-to-Action Examples That Convert (and Why They Work)
desc: Call-to-action examples for small business websites, with guidance on wording, placement, design and matching the CTA to where the visitor is in their decision.
cat: design
kw: call to action examples, cta examples, website call to action, cta button text
takeaways: Good CTAs tell visitors exactly what they'll get when they click | Start with a verb and add a benefit or reassurance | Repeat one primary CTA throughout the page | Match the ask to the visitor's readiness: low-commitment first, bigger later
---
A call to action is the moment your website asks for something. Get it wrong and visitors admire your site and leave. Get it right and the next step feels obvious. Small changes in wording and placement can make a surprising difference.

## What makes a CTA work

1. **Clarity:** it's obvious what happens next
2. **Value:** it promises something the visitor wants
3. **Low risk:** it doesn't feel like a trap
4. **Visibility:** it stands out
5. **Consistency:** the same ask, repeated

## Wording formulas

### Verb + what they get

- Get my free quote
- Book a free call
- Check your date
- See my savings
- Download the guide

### Add reassurance

- Get a free website preview (no card required)
- Book a call (30 minutes, no pressure)
- Request a quote (reply within 24 hours)

### First person

"Get **my** free quote" often outperforms "Get **your** free quote" because the visitor reads it as their own action.

## Examples by industry

| Business | Primary CTA |
| --- | --- |
| Coach | Book your free discovery call |
| Photographer | Check your date |
| Solar installer | Get my free solar quote |
| Mortgage broker | Book a free 30-minute call |
| Salon | Book now |
| Dentist | Book an appointment |
| Trades | Get a free quote / Call now |
| Restaurant | Book a table |
| SaaS | Start free |
| Web studio | Get a free website preview |

## Words to avoid

- **Submit:** says nothing about the benefit
- **Click here:** vague
- **Learn more:** fine as a secondary link, weak as the main ask
- **Contact us:** functional but uninspiring; say why

## Placement

- **Header:** always visible
- **Hero:** the first thing after the headline
- **After each major section:** especially proof
- **End of page:** for people who've read everything
- **Sticky bar on mobile:** always one tap away

Don't make people scroll back up to act.

## Design

- **Contrast:** the button colour should stand out from everything around it
- **Size:** easy to tap; at least 44 pixels high
- **Space:** room around it
- **Shape:** looks like a button
- **One primary style:** secondary actions get a quieter treatment

## Primary and secondary

Offer a main action and a lower-commitment alternative:

- **Primary:** Get a free quote
- **Secondary:** See our work

The secondary catches people who aren't ready, without competing.

## Match the ask to the stage

| Visitor stage | Good CTA |
| --- | --- |
| Just browsing | Read the guide, see examples |
| Comparing | View pricing, see case studies |
| Ready | Book, buy, get a quote |

A blog reader may not be ready to book. Offer a guide or a free audit instead.

## Supporting text

A short line under the button can lift clicks:

- "Takes 60 seconds"
- "No obligation"
- "We reply within one business day"
- "Join 500+ happy customers"

[[cta]]

## Test it

Change one element at a time (wording, colour, position) and compare enquiries over a few weeks. Start with wording; it's usually the biggest lever.

## Beyond buttons

- **Forms** are CTAs: label them with what the visitor gets
- **Phone numbers:** "Call us now on..." with tap-to-call
- **WhatsApp:** "Message us on WhatsApp"
- **Exit offers:** a gentle prompt for a guide before someone leaves

For the bigger picture, see [how to get more leads from your website](/blog/website-conversion-rate-optimization/).

## Frequently asked questions

### How many CTAs should a page have?
One primary action, repeated several times, plus an optional secondary.

### What colour should a CTA button be?
Whichever contrasts most with your palette and is used only for actions.

### Should CTAs be above the fold?
Yes, at least one. Then repeat further down.

=== mobile-first-design
title: Mobile-First Web Design: Why It Matters and How to Do It
desc: What mobile-first web design means, why it matters for rankings and conversions, and practical guidelines for layouts, navigation, forms and speed on phones.
cat: design
kw: mobile first design, mobile friendly website, responsive web design, mobile website best practices
takeaways: Most visitors and Google's indexing both use the mobile version of your site | Designing for the small screen first forces clarity | Thumb-friendly buttons, short forms and tap-to-call lift mobile conversions | Speed on mobile data is the real test
---
For most small businesses, more than half of website visits come from phones, often far more for local and social traffic. Google also indexes and ranks the mobile version of your site. Yet many sites are still designed on a big monitor and squeezed down as an afterthought.

Mobile-first flips that: design for the phone, then expand for larger screens.

## Why mobile-first

- **Your customers are there.** Searching on the sofa, in the car park, between meetings.
- **Google uses mobile-first indexing.** If content is missing on mobile, it may as well not exist.
- **Constraints create clarity.** A small screen forces you to prioritise.
- **Conversions.** A frustrating mobile experience loses the enquiry.

## Layout

- **Single column.** Stack content vertically.
- **Most important things first:** headline, key benefit, call to action.
- **Generous spacing** between tappable elements.
- **No horizontal scrolling.**
- **Readable type:** body text around 16 to 18 pixels.

## Navigation

- A clear menu button, or a short visible menu
- Five to seven items at most
- A sticky header with your logo and primary action
- A tappable phone number

## Buttons and tap targets

- At least 44 × 44 pixels
- Space between links so thumbs don't hit the wrong one
- Primary buttons full-width or close to it
- Place key actions within easy thumb reach

## Forms

- As few fields as possible
- The right keyboard for each field (number pad for phone, email keyboard for email)
- Autofill enabled
- Large inputs and labels above fields
- Clear error messages

See [contact forms that convert](/blog/contact-form-best-practices/).

## Images and media

- Serve smaller images to smaller screens
- Lazy-load below the first screen
- Avoid text baked into images; it becomes unreadable
- Don't autoplay heavy video on mobile data

See our [image SEO guide](/blog/image-seo/).

## Speed

Phones have slower processors and connections than laptops. Test on a mid-range phone using mobile data, not office Wi-Fi.

- Minimise JavaScript
- Compress everything
- Limit third-party scripts
- Aim for good Core Web Vitals on mobile ([explained](/blog/core-web-vitals-explained/))

## Mobile-specific features

- **Tap to call**
- **Tap for directions**
- **WhatsApp or SMS links**
- **Sticky call-to-action bar**
- **Apple Pay / Google Pay** at checkout

## Content parity

Don't hide important content on mobile. Since Google indexes the mobile version, anything removed there is effectively removed from search. Use accordions and tabs to save space without deleting.

## Pop-ups

Full-screen pop-ups on mobile are irritating and can be penalised by Google if they block content on arrival. If you use them, keep them small, easy to dismiss and delayed.

[[audit]]

## How to test

1. Open your site on several real phones
2. Try to complete your main action with one thumb
3. Use Chrome DevTools' device mode for a quick check
4. Run PageSpeed Insights on mobile
5. Ask a friend to find your phone number and send an enquiry while you watch

## Responsive vs mobile-first

Responsive design means a site adapts to any screen. Mobile-first is the mindset of designing for small screens before large ones. You want both: a responsive site, designed mobile-first.

## Common mobile mistakes

- Tiny text and links
- Menus that don't work
- Buttons hidden behind chat widgets
- Tables that overflow the screen
- Forms asking for too much
- Slow hero videos

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a separate mobile site?
No. One responsive site is the standard and what Google recommends.

### What about tablets?
A good responsive design adapts naturally. Test at tablet widths too.

### Is desktop still important?
Yes, especially for B2B. Mobile-first doesn't mean desktop-last; it means starting with the hardest constraint.

=== website-accessibility-basics
title: Web Accessibility Basics: Make Your Website Usable for Everyone
desc: A practical introduction to web accessibility for small businesses: why it matters, the most important fixes and how to test your site.
cat: design
kw: web accessibility basics, website accessibility checklist, wcag small business, accessible website design
takeaways: Accessibility helps people with disabilities and improves the experience for everyone | Good contrast, alt text, keyboard access and clear structure solve most common issues | Accessible sites tend to perform better in search | Legal requirements are growing, so it's wise to build accessibly from the start
---
Roughly one in six people worldwide lives with a significant disability. Many more have temporary or situational limitations: a broken wrist, bright sunlight on a screen, a baby in one arm. An accessible website works for all of them. It's the right thing to do, it widens your audience, and in many countries it's a legal expectation.

## What accessibility means

An accessible website can be used by people who:

- Are blind or have low vision
- Are deaf or hard of hearing
- Have limited mobility and may use a keyboard or switch
- Have cognitive or learning differences
- Are sensitive to motion or flashing

The international standard is the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG). Level AA is the usual target.

## The fixes that matter most

### 1. Colour contrast

Text must stand out from its background. Aim for a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for body text. Pale grey text on white is a common failure.

### 2. Alt text for images

Describe meaningful images so screen readers can convey them. Decorative images should have empty alt text. See [image SEO](/blog/image-seo/).

### 3. Keyboard navigation

Everything should work without a mouse: menus, forms, buttons, pop-ups. Tab through your site and check you can see where you are (the focus indicator) and reach everything.

### 4. Clear heading structure

One H1, then H2s and H3s in order. Screen reader users navigate by headings. It helps SEO too.

### 5. Descriptive links and buttons

"Read our pricing guide" tells users where they're going. "Click here" doesn't.

### 6. Form labels and errors

Every field needs a visible label. Errors should say what's wrong and how to fix it, not only turn red.

### 7. Text size and spacing

Use readable sizes, allow zooming, and keep line length and spacing comfortable.

### 8. Captions and transcripts

Videos need captions. Audio needs a transcript.

### 9. Don't rely on colour alone

"Fields in red are required" fails for colour-blind users. Add text or icons.

### 10. Respect motion preferences

Some people feel unwell with heavy animation. Honour the "reduce motion" setting on their device and avoid flashing content. This site, for example, switches off its animations when that setting is on.

### 11. Tap targets

Buttons and links large enough to tap accurately.

### 12. Language and page titles

Set the page language in the code and give every page a unique, descriptive title.

## Why it's good for business

- **Larger audience**
- **Better SEO:** structure, alt text and clear content help search engines
- **Better usability for everyone**
- **Reduced legal risk**
- **Stronger brand**

## Legal landscape

Rules vary. The ADA in the United States, the Equality Act in the UK, the European Accessibility Act in the EU and similar laws elsewhere increasingly apply to websites. If you serve the public, treat accessibility as a requirement. For specific obligations, take legal advice.

## How to test

1. **Keyboard test:** unplug the mouse and use Tab, Enter and arrow keys
2. **Automated checks:** WAVE, axe DevTools or Lighthouse catch many issues
3. **Contrast checker:** WebAIM's tool
4. **Zoom to 200%:** does the layout still work?
5. **Screen reader:** try VoiceOver (Mac, iPhone) or NVDA (Windows)

Automated tools find perhaps a third of issues. Manual checks matter.

[[audit]]

## Be wary of overlay widgets

Plugins that promise instant compliance with one line of code don't fix underlying problems and are widely criticised by accessibility experts. Build it properly.

## Build it in from the start

Accessibility is far cheaper to include in a new design than to retrofit. Ask any designer you hire how they handle it; it's a good test of quality. See [how to choose a web designer](/blog/how-to-choose-a-web-designer/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is accessibility expensive?
Not when it's part of the build. Most essentials are simply good practice.

### Does accessibility limit design?
No. Beautiful, distinctive sites can be fully accessible. It requires care, not compromise.

### Do small businesses really need to worry?
Yes. Legal expectations aside, you're turning away customers if they can't use your site.
