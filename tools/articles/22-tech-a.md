Customers ask the same questions again and again: what are your prices, are you open Saturday, do you cover my area, can I book for Thursday? An AI chatbot can answer those instantly, at 2am, in several languages, and pass the serious enquiries to you with the details already collected.

We build chatbots and run our own chatbot product, CustomBotify. Here's a clear-eyed look at what they do well.

## What a modern AI chatbot is

Earlier chatbots followed rigid scripts: press 1 for hours. Today's are powered by large language models and can understand natural questions, then answer from a knowledge base you provide: your website, FAQs, price list, policies and documents.

## What they're good at

### Answering common questions

Hours, prices, services, policies, delivery, how things work.

### Capturing leads

Asking for a name, email and what the visitor needs, then sending it to your inbox or CRM.

### Qualifying enquiries

"What's your budget? When do you need it? Where are you based?" so you spend time on the right ones.

### Booking

Connecting to your calendar to offer available times.

### Supporting customers

Order status, how-to help, returns.

### Working across channels

The same assistant on your website, WhatsApp, Instagram and Messenger.

## What they're not good at

- **Judgement calls** on complex or sensitive matters
- **Anything they haven't been given** information about
- **Replacing human relationships** in high-trust sales
- **Regulated advice** (medical, legal, financial) without strict controls

A good chatbot knows its limits and hands over gracefully.

## How it learns about your business

1. **You supply content:** website pages, documents, FAQs
2. **The system indexes it** so the AI can retrieve relevant passages
3. **When asked a question,** it finds the right information and writes an answer based on it
4. **You refine:** review conversations, fix gaps, add content

This approach keeps answers grounded in your material and makes updates easy: change the document and the bot's answers change too.

## Guardrails that matter

- **Stick to your content.** Instruct the bot to say "I'm not sure, let me connect you with the team" when it doesn't know.
- **Disclose that it's AI.** Honesty builds trust and is increasingly a legal requirement.
- **Offer a human handover:** email, phone, WhatsApp or live chat.
- **Protect privacy:** don't collect sensitive data in chat, and mention the chatbot in your privacy policy.
- **Review transcripts** regularly, especially at first.

## Costs

| Option | Typical cost |
| --- | --- |
| No-code chatbot platform | $20 to $150 a month |
| Custom-built assistant with integrations | $1,500 to $10,000+ to build, plus usage |
| AI usage fees | Usually cents per conversation |

For most small businesses a no-code tool is the right starting point.

## Where to start

Pick one job:

1. **FAQ assistant:** deflect repetitive questions
2. **Lead capture:** engage visitors who'd otherwise leave
3. **Booking helper:** turn interest into appointments

Launch, watch real conversations for two weeks, improve, then expand.

## Adding it to your site

- A chat bubble in the corner, loaded after the page so it doesn't slow things down
- A welcome message that says what it can help with
- Suggested questions to get people started
- A clear route to a human

Mind the placement: don't cover your call-to-action buttons or WhatsApp link on mobile.

## Measuring success

- Conversations started
- Questions answered without human help
- Leads captured
- Bookings made
- Customer satisfaction

If visitors need a bot to find your prices or opening hours, fix the site too. The best results come from a clear site plus an assistant for everything else. See [how to get more leads from your website](/blog/website-conversion-rate-optimization/).

## Beyond the website

The same technology can work behind the scenes: drafting replies, summarising enquiries, updating your CRM. See [15 automation ideas for small businesses](/blog/business-automation-ideas/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Will a chatbot annoy my customers?
Not if it's helpful, honest about being AI and easy to bypass for a human.

### Can it speak other languages?
Yes. Modern models handle many languages well, which is valuable for diverse customer bases.

### Is my data safe?
Choose providers with clear data policies, avoid collecting sensitive information in chat and check where data is stored.

Every small business has tasks that are done the same way, over and over: copying enquiry details into a spreadsheet, sending the same reply, chasing invoices, asking for reviews. Each takes minutes. Together they take days.

Automation hands those tasks to software. Here are fifteen that pay back quickly.

## Leads and sales

### 1. Instant enquiry replies

When someone submits your form, send an immediate personalised email or text confirming receipt and next steps. Speed wins business.

### 2. Lead capture to CRM

Form submissions, chatbot leads and missed calls go straight into your CRM or a spreadsheet, tagged with their source.

### 3. Follow-up sequences

If a lead hasn't replied in two days, a friendly follow-up goes out automatically. Then another after a week.

### 4. Quote and proposal generation

Fill a template from form answers and send a branded quote in minutes.

### 5. Lead notifications

A text or app alert for every new enquiry, so someone responds while the customer is still on your site.

## Booking and scheduling

### 6. Online booking

Customers choose a time; your calendar updates; nobody plays phone tag. See [online booking for small businesses](/blog/online-booking-for-small-business/).

### 7. Reminders

Automatic email and SMS reminders reduce no-shows significantly.

### 8. Rescheduling links

Let customers move appointments themselves.

## Money

### 9. Invoicing

Generate and send invoices when a job is marked complete or a booking is made.

### 10. Payment reminders

Polite, automatic nudges for overdue invoices.

### 11. Online payments

Take deposits and payments through your website with Stripe or similar. See [accepting payments on your website](/blog/stripe-payments-website/).

## Customers

### 12. Review requests

A message asking for a Google review, sent a day or two after a job or delivery, with a direct link.

### 13. Onboarding

A welcome email, a checklist and the forms new clients need, sent the moment they sign up.

### 14. Customer support answers

An AI assistant handles common questions and escalates the rest. See [AI chatbots for small business](/blog/ai-chatbot-for-small-business/).

## Marketing

### 15. Email nurture

New subscribers receive a short series introducing your business, sharing tips and inviting them to take the next step.

## Bonus ideas

- Social posts scheduled in batches
- New blog posts shared automatically
- Birthday or anniversary messages
- Stock alerts and reordering
- Weekly performance summaries

## How to start

1. **List repetitive tasks** you do weekly
2. **Estimate time** spent on each
3. **Pick one** with a clear trigger and a clear action
4. **Build it,** test it and watch it for a week
5. **Move to the next**

Begin with lead response. It usually has the biggest impact on revenue.

## Keep the human touch

Automate the routine, not the relationship. A personal call after an automated confirmation, a handwritten note with an automated invoice. Customers notice.

## Pitfalls

- **Over-automation:** robotic messages that feel cold
- **No testing:** broken automations fail silently
- **No owner:** someone needs to check they still work
- **Ignoring privacy rules** for marketing messages

### Is automation expensive?
Many automations cost little or nothing using tools you already have. Custom work costs more and pays back through time saved.

### Do I need technical skills?
Not for no-code tools like Zapier. More complex integrations benefit from a developer.

### What should I automate first?
Whatever you do most often that follows the same steps every time. For most businesses: responding to enquiries.

"We should have an app" is a tempting thought. Apps feel modern and sit on the customer's home screen. They're also a significant investment, and many businesses that build one would have been better served by a great website.

Here's how to decide.

## What each is best at

| | Website | Mobile app |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reach | Anyone with a browser | Only those who install it |
| Discovery | Search engines, links, ads | App stores, word of mouth |
| Cost | Lower | Higher |
| Updates | Instant | Via app store review |
| Offline use | Limited | Strong |
| Device features | Some | Full: camera, GPS, sensors, notifications |
| Speed and feel | Good | Excellent |
| Best for | Being found, informing, converting | Frequent, repeated use |

## You almost certainly need a website

- It's how new customers find you
- It ranks on Google
- It requires no download
- It's where people check you're legitimate

Even app-first companies have websites to explain and promote the app.

## When an app makes sense

Ask: will customers open this **at least weekly**?

Strong cases:

- **Loyalty and repeat ordering:** cafés, salons, gyms, retailers
- **Bookings with accounts:** classes, clinics, memberships
- **On-demand services:** delivery, transport, field services
- **Communities and learning:** courses, coaching programmes, social features
- **Tools:** trackers, calculators, productivity
- **Offline or device-heavy use:** scanning, photos, location, sensors
- **Internal apps** for staff

Weak cases:

- A brochure for your business
- One-off purchases
- Content that changes rarely
- "Because competitors have one"

## The cost reality

| | Typical cost |
| --- | --- |
| Business website | $2,500 to $10,000 |
| Simple app (MVP) | $15,000 to $40,000 |
| Complex app | $50,000 to $250,000+ |
| App maintenance | 15 to 20% of build cost per year |

Apps also need store accounts, review compliance and updates for each new operating system release. See [how much does a mobile app cost?](/blog/react-native-app-cost/)

## The middle ground: progressive web apps

A progressive web app (PWA) is a website with app-like abilities:

- Can be added to the home screen
- Works offline or on poor connections
- Loads fast
- Can send push notifications on supported devices
- No app store required

For many businesses a PWA delivers most of what they wanted from an app at a fraction of the cost.

## Cross-platform apps

If you do need a real app, frameworks like React Native let one codebase run on both iOS and Android, cutting cost and time compared with building each separately. It's our default at Code Bunny.

## A decision checklist

- [ ] Will customers use it weekly or more?
- [ ] Do we need device features a website can't access?
- [ ] Do we need offline use?
- [ ] Would notifications genuinely help customers?
- [ ] Do we have budget for build **and** ongoing maintenance?
- [ ] Do we have a plan to get people to install it?

Mostly yes: consider an app. Mostly no: invest in a better website.

## Start with the web, then graduate

A common, sensible path:

1. Build a strong, mobile-first website
2. Add web-app features (accounts, booking, payments)
3. Watch what customers use repeatedly
4. Build a mobile app around proven behaviour

This avoids spending heavily on an app nobody opens.

Building an app is half the job. You'll need to promote it: on your website, in store, by email, with QR codes and incentives to install. Without that, it sits unused.

Yes, through web push on most modern browsers and devices, with the user's permission.

No. Websites are indexed by search engines; app content mostly isn't.

App pricing is confusing because "an app" can mean a simple booking tool or the next ride-sharing platform. The useful question isn't "how much does an app cost?" but "how much does *this* app cost, and what's the smallest version worth building first?"

## Typical ranges

| App type | Typical cost | Timeline |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simple MVP (few screens, basic features) | $15,000 to $40,000 | 6 to 12 weeks |
| Mid-complexity (accounts, payments, admin) | $40,000 to $100,000 | 3 to 6 months |
| Complex (real-time, marketplace, heavy integrations) | $100,000 to $250,000+ | 6 to 12 months+ |

Rates vary considerably by region and team. A senior studio in a lower-cost country can deliver the same quality for less than a large agency in a major city.

## What drives the cost

### 1. Features

Each feature is a mini project. Common ones:

- User accounts and login
- Profiles and settings
- Payments and subscriptions
- Push notifications
- Chat or messaging
- Maps and location
- Camera, scanning, media upload
- Search and filters
- Offline mode
- Admin dashboard

### 2. Platforms

Building separate native apps for iOS and Android roughly doubles the work. Cross-platform frameworks avoid that.

### 3. Design

Custom, polished interfaces with animation take longer than standard components. Good design is worth it; it's what users judge first.

### 4. Backend

Most apps need a server: database, APIs, authentication, file storage. Often this is as much work as the app itself.

### 5. Integrations

Payment providers, CRMs, booking systems, mapping services, analytics.

### 6. Compliance and security

Health, finance or children's apps carry extra requirements.

## How React Native reduces cost

React Native lets developers write one codebase that runs as a real app on both iOS and Android.

- **One team, one codebase**
- **Faster development**
- **Shared updates** across platforms
- **Near-native performance** for most apps

It's why it's our default for client apps. Fully native development still makes sense for some graphics-heavy or highly specialised apps.

## The costs people forget

| Item | Typical cost |
| --- | --- |
| Apple Developer account | $99 a year |
| Google Play account | $25 one-off |
| Hosting and backend services | $20 to $500+ a month |
| Third-party services (maps, SMS, email) | Usage-based |
| Maintenance and updates | 15 to 20% of build cost a year |
| App store fees on digital sales | Up to 15 to 30% |
| Marketing | Often more than the build |

## Why maintenance matters

Operating systems update every year. Devices change. Libraries need patching. An app left alone slowly breaks. Budget for it from day one.

## How to keep the budget under control

### Build an MVP

A minimum viable product has only the features needed to deliver the core value. Launch it, learn from real users, then invest in what they actually use.

### Prioritise ruthlessly

Sort features into must-have, should-have and later. Launch with the first list.

### Use existing services

Authentication, payments, notifications and chat can be handled by proven services so you're not building them from scratch.

### Prototype first

A clickable prototype costs little and catches expensive misunderstandings before development begins.

### Choose cross-platform

Unless you have a specific reason not to.
