=== website-copywriting-guide
title: Website Copywriting for Small Businesses: Words That Win Customers
desc: A practical guide to writing website copy for a small business: headlines, service pages, about pages, calls to action and editing tips that lift conversions.
cat: design
kw: website copywriting, small business website copy, how to write website content, web copy tips
takeaways: Write about the customer's problem and outcome before you write about yourself | Clear beats clever; use the words your customers use | Break text into short sections with meaningful headings | Every page should lead to one next step
---
Visitors don't read websites the way they read books. They scan, looking for a reason to stay or go. Good web copy makes the right things jump out: what you do, why it matters to them, why they should trust you and what to do next.

You don't need to be a writer. You need to be clear.

## Start with the customer

Before writing, answer:

- Who is this page for?
- What problem brought them here?
- What do they want to happen?
- What might stop them choosing you?

Then write to one person, using "you".

## Headlines

Your headline is the most-read line on the page. Make it specific.

| Vague | Clear |
| --- | --- |
| Welcome to our website | Emergency plumbing in Leeds, 24 hours a day |
| Quality you can trust | Kitchens designed, built and fitted in six weeks |
| Solutions for your business | Bookkeeping for freelancers, from £49 a month |

A good test: could this headline appear on a competitor's site? If yes, sharpen it.

## Benefits before features

A feature is what something is. A benefit is what it does for the customer.

- Feature: "24-panel 9.6 kW system"
- Benefit: "Cut your electricity bill by up to 70%"

Lead with the benefit, support with the feature.

## Use their words

Listen to how customers describe their needs in emails, reviews and calls. Use those phrases. If they say "leaky tap", don't write "faucet malfunction".

## Structure for scanning

- **Short paragraphs:** one to three sentences
- **Headings** that summarise each section
- **Bullets** for lists
- **Bold** for the key phrase
- **Front-load:** put the important words at the start of sentences and headings

## Page by page

### Homepage

Headline, who you help, services overview, proof, how it works, call to action. See [homepage design best practices](/blog/homepage-design-best-practices/).

### Service pages

- Who it's for and the problem
- What you do and what's included
- How it works
- Price guidance
- Proof
- FAQs
- Call to action

### About

Why you do this, who's behind the business and why customers can trust you. Tell it as a story with a point.

### Contact

Reassure: how quickly you reply and what happens next.

## Be specific

Specifics persuade. Generalities don't.

- "Many happy customers" → "186 five-star Google reviews"
- "Fast turnaround" → "Most sites live in two weeks"
- "Experienced team" → "17 years installing solar in Southern California"

## Cut the filler

Delete:

- "We are passionate about"
- "State-of-the-art"
- "One-stop shop"
- "Tailored solutions"
- "Going the extra mile"

If a sentence could be removed without losing meaning, remove it.

## Write like you talk

Read your copy aloud. If it sounds stiff, rewrite it the way you'd say it to a customer across a table.

## Calls to action

Tell people what to do, and what they'll get. "Get my free quote" beats "Submit". More in [call-to-action examples](/blog/call-to-action-examples/).

[[cta]]

## Writing for search

- Include the phrases people search in titles, headings and opening paragraphs
- Answer real questions
- Don't stuff keywords; write naturally

See [keyword research for small businesses](/blog/keyword-research-small-business/).

## Proof in words

Let customers do the talking. A short testimonial beside a claim makes it believable.

## Editing checklist

- [ ] Is the main point clear in five seconds?
- [ ] Are there more "you"s than "we"s?
- [ ] Is every claim specific or backed up?
- [ ] Are paragraphs short?
- [ ] Is there one clear next step?
- [ ] Have I read it aloud?
- [ ] Is it free of typos?

## Using AI as a helper

AI tools can outline, suggest headlines and tidy grammar. Left alone, they produce generic copy that sounds like everyone else. Feed them your real customer language and edit hard.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much text should a page have?
Enough to answer the visitor's questions. Service pages usually need 400 to 900 words; homepages can be similar, broken into clear sections.

### Should I hire a copywriter?
If writing isn't your strength, yes. Good copy pays for itself in conversions. Many web designers, including us, include copy support.

### How often should I update copy?
Whenever your services, prices or proof change, and review everything at least yearly.

=== landing-page-vs-website
title: Landing Page vs Website: What's the Difference and Which Do You Need?
desc: The difference between a landing page and a full website, when to use each, and what a high-converting landing page should include.
cat: design
kw: landing page vs website, what is a landing page, landing page for small business, one page website
takeaways: A website serves many purposes; a landing page serves one | Use landing pages for ads, campaigns and specific offers | Remove navigation and distractions so the only choice is to act | Most businesses need both
---
"Do I need a website or just a landing page?" is a common question, and the answer is usually "both, for different jobs". Understanding the difference helps you spend your marketing budget where it works.

## The difference in one sentence

A **website** is your whole shop: multiple pages for different visitors and purposes. A **landing page** is a single stand built for one audience, one offer and one action.

| | Website | Landing page |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pages | Many | One |
| Purpose | Inform, build trust, rank, convert | Convert for one specific offer |
| Navigation | Full menu | Minimal or none |
| Traffic | Search, direct, referrals | Ads, email, social campaigns |
| Calls to action | Several | One |
| SEO | Core purpose | Usually secondary |

## When you need a website

- You want to be found on Google for a range of searches
- You offer several services or serve several audiences
- Customers research you before buying
- You need pages like about, pricing, portfolio and blog
- You want a long-term asset

For nearly every established business, a website is the foundation.

## When you need a landing page

- You're running Google, Facebook or Instagram ads
- You're launching a product, event or promotion
- You're promoting a lead magnet or webinar
- You want to test an offer quickly
- You're a new business validating an idea

Sending ad traffic to your homepage wastes money. A homepage offers ten paths; a landing page offers one.

## Anatomy of a high-converting landing page

1. **Headline** that matches the ad or email that brought them
2. **Subheadline** adding the key benefit
3. **Hero image or video** showing the outcome
4. **Call to action** above the fold
5. **Benefits:** three to five, focused on outcomes
6. **Proof:** testimonials, ratings, logos, numbers
7. **How it works:** simple steps
8. **Objection handling:** FAQ, guarantee
9. **Urgency,** if genuine: limited places, deadline
10. **Final call to action**

No main menu. No links elsewhere. The only way forward is the action you want.

## Message match

The words in your ad should appear on the landing page. If the ad says "Free solar quote in Barrie", the page should say exactly that. Mismatch causes visitors to bounce.

## One page, one goal

Decide the single action: book a call, request a quote, buy, download. Remove anything that doesn't support it.

## Examples

LaunchMint is a single high-conversion checkout page with a countdown, social proof and one-click purchase. Our own [free website preview](/free-website-preview/) page is built the same way: one offer, one form.

[[work:launchmint,thrill-lands]]

## A one-page website is a third thing

A one-page website presents a whole business on a single scrolling page with a menu that jumps to sections. It suits new or simple businesses. The trade-off is SEO: one page can rank for only a few terms. See [what pages should a small business website have?](/blog/what-pages-should-a-small-business-website-have/)

## How they work together

- **Website** builds organic visibility and credibility
- **Landing pages** convert paid and campaign traffic
- Both share branding, proof and tracking

Typical setup: a main site plus a landing page for each campaign or service you advertise.

[[cta]]

## Measuring a landing page

- **Conversion rate:** actions ÷ visitors
- **Cost per lead:** ad spend ÷ leads
- **Bounce rate** and scroll depth

Test one change at a time: headline, image, form length, button text. See [how to get more leads from your website](/blog/website-conversion-rate-optimization/).

## Should landing pages be indexed?

Campaign-specific pages are often set to noindex so they don't compete with your main pages. Evergreen ones targeting a keyword can be indexed.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I start with just a landing page?
Yes, to validate an idea or run a first campaign. Plan to build a full site as you grow.

### How long should a landing page be?
As long as it takes to persuade. Simple, low-cost offers need less; considered purchases need more.

### Do I need a different landing page for each ad?
Ideally one per offer or audience, so the message matches precisely.

=== website-trust-signals
title: 20 Website Trust Signals That Turn Visitors into Customers
desc: Twenty trust signals every small business website should consider, from reviews and guarantees to real photos and secure checkout, and where to place them.
cat: design
kw: website trust signals, build trust on website, website credibility, social proof examples
takeaways: Visitors decide whether to trust you within seconds | Reviews, real photos and clear contact details are the foundation | Place proof beside calls to action, where doubt is highest | Professional design, speed and accuracy are trust signals too
---
A stranger lands on your website. They don't know you, can't see your premises and have heard stories about businesses that didn't deliver. Before they'll enquire, they need reasons to believe you're real, competent and safe to deal with.

Trust signals are those reasons. Here are twenty, grouped by type.

## Proof from others

### 1. Reviews and ratings

Show your Google rating and review count prominently. "5.0 from 186 reviews" is hard to argue with.

### 2. Testimonials

Named quotes with photos, roles or locations. Specific results beat general praise.

### 3. Case studies

The problem, what you did and the outcome, with numbers.

### 4. Client logos

Recognisable names lend credibility. Get permission.

### 5. Real messages

Screenshots of genuine feedback, shared with consent, feel authentic because nobody wrote them for marketing. Our homepage features a WhatsApp from a client that says "I got a lot of compliments on it."

### 6. Numbers

Years in business, customers served, projects completed. Be accurate.

### 7. Press and awards

"As featured in" logos, industry awards and rankings.

## Proof of legitimacy

### 8. Full contact details

Address, phone number, email. A contact form alone raises suspicion.

### 9. Real photos

Your team, premises, vehicles and work. Stock photos of smiling strangers do the opposite of building trust.

### 10. About page with real people

Names, faces and a story.

### 11. Accreditations and licences

Trade bodies, professional registrations, certifications, with numbers where applicable.

### 12. Business registration details

Company number, tax ID or regulatory status in the footer.

### 13. Insurance

Especially for trades and professional services.

## Reducing risk

### 14. Guarantees

Money-back, satisfaction or workmanship guarantees, stated clearly.

### 15. Free trials, samples or previews

Let people experience your work before committing. It's why we offer a [free website preview](/free-website-preview/).

### 16. Clear pricing

Prices, ranges or "from" figures show you have nothing to hide.

### 17. Transparent policies

Returns, cancellations, privacy and terms that are easy to find and read.

## Technical trust

### 18. HTTPS

The padlock. Without it, browsers warn visitors your site isn't secure.

### 19. Secure payment badges

Recognised payment logos at checkout reassure buyers.

### 20. A fast, polished, up-to-date site

Typos, broken links, slow pages and a 2019 copyright date all erode confidence. Quality of execution is itself a signal.

## Where to place them

| Location | Best signals |
| --- | --- |
| Above the fold | Rating, key number, top accreditation |
| Beside calls to action | Testimonial, guarantee, "no obligation" |
| Service pages | Relevant case study and reviews |
| Checkout / forms | Security badges, privacy reassurance |
| Footer | Contact details, registrations, all accreditations |

Repeat the strongest ones. Visitors don't see every section.

## Match signals to objections

- "Are they any good?" → reviews, portfolio
- "Are they legitimate?" → address, registration, photos
- "Will it work for me?" → case studies from similar customers
- "What if it goes wrong?" → guarantee, policies
- "Is it safe to pay?" → HTTPS, payment badges

[[cta]]

## Keep it honest

- Never invent reviews or use stock photos as "customers"
- Keep numbers current
- Only show accreditations you hold
- Remove outdated awards

Fake trust signals destroy trust permanently when discovered, and may be illegal.

## Industry notes

- **Solar:** [trust signals every solar website needs](/blog/solar-website-trust-signals/)
- **Coaches:** [how to use testimonials](/blog/coaching-testimonials/)
- **Wedding vendors:** [how to get more reviews](/blog/reviews-for-wedding-vendors/)

## Frequently asked questions

### Which trust signal matters most?
For most small businesses, genuine reviews with a visible count and rating.

### What if I'm a new business with no reviews?
Use your personal credentials, guarantees, real photos and a few early testimonials, even from beta or discounted clients.

### Can there be too many trust signals?
Yes, if they clutter the page. Choose the strongest and place them well.

=== contact-form-best-practices
title: Contact Forms That Convert: 15 Best Practices
desc: Fifteen contact form best practices for small business websites: fewer fields, clearer labels, mobile-friendly design, spam protection and better follow-up.
cat: design
kw: contact form best practices, contact form design, website form conversion, lead form tips
takeaways: Every extra field reduces completions; ask only what you need | Label the button with what the visitor gets | Reassure about privacy and response time next to the form | Test your own form regularly, because broken forms lose leads silently
---
The contact form is where interest becomes a lead. It's also where a surprising number of websites lose people: too many fields, vague buttons, forms that don't work on phones, or submissions that vanish into a spam folder.

These fifteen practices fix most of it.

## 1. Ask for less

Start with the minimum: name, email (or phone) and message. Add fields only if you'll genuinely use the answer to respond better.

## 2. Make optional fields optional

Mark them clearly. Don't force a phone number if email will do.

## 3. Use a single column

Easier to scan and far better on mobile.

## 4. Put labels above fields

Visible labels beat placeholder-only text, which disappears when someone starts typing and causes accessibility problems.

## 5. Use the right input types

Email fields bring up the email keyboard; phone fields bring up the number pad; dates get a date picker. Small things that make mobile completion much easier.

## 6. Enable autofill

Let browsers fill in name, email and phone. Fewer keystrokes, more submissions.

## 7. Write a specific button

"Send message", "Get my free quote", "Request a callback". Never just "Submit". See [call-to-action examples](/blog/call-to-action-examples/).

## 8. Set expectations

Next to the form: "We reply within one business day." People are more willing to enquire when they know what happens next.

## 9. Reassure about privacy

"We'll never share your details." Link to your privacy policy.

## 10. Use multi-step for longer forms

If you need more information (quotes, applications), split it into steps with a progress indicator. Start with easy questions.

## 11. Show clear errors

Tell people exactly what's wrong, next to the field, as they go. Don't clear the form if there's a mistake.

## 12. Confirm success

After submission, show a clear thank-you message or page explaining next steps. Send a confirmation email too.

## 13. Stop spam without annoying humans

- A hidden "honeypot" field bots fill in and humans don't see
- Invisible verification services
- Avoid image puzzles where possible

## 14. Offer alternatives

Beside the form, list phone, email and WhatsApp. Some people will never fill in a form. See [adding WhatsApp to your website](/blog/whatsapp-for-business-website/).

## 15. Test it regularly

Submit your own form monthly. Check the email arrives, and not in spam. A broken form can go unnoticed for weeks while leads disappear.

## Where to put forms

- A dedicated contact page
- At the end of service pages
- In the footer or a final section on the homepage
- On landing pages, above the fold

Don't make interested visitors hunt for it.

## What to ask, by business type

| Business | Useful fields |
| --- | --- |
| Service business | Name, phone, postcode, service needed |
| Coach | Name, email, what you'd like help with |
| Photographer | Names, date, venue, message |
| Solar installer | Postcode, monthly bill, phone |
| B2B | Name, company, email, requirement |

Industry specifics: [wedding inquiry forms](/blog/wedding-inquiry-form/) and [solar quote forms](/blog/solar-quote-form/).

[[cta]]

## Route submissions properly

- Send to an inbox that's monitored
- Add to your CRM automatically
- Notify by SMS or app for urgent enquiries
- Record the page the enquiry came from

## Follow up fast

Speed of reply is one of the biggest factors in winning the business. Aim for minutes during working hours, and the same day at the latest.

## Track conversions

Record form submissions as conversions in analytics so you know which pages and traffic sources generate leads. See [how to get more leads from your website](/blog/website-conversion-rate-optimization/).

## Accessibility

Labels, keyboard access, visible focus and clear errors make forms usable for everyone. See [web accessibility basics](/blog/website-accessibility-basics/).

## Frequently asked questions

### How many fields is too many?
For a first enquiry, more than five or six starts to hurt. Ask the rest later.

### Should I require a phone number?
Only if a call is essential to your process. Otherwise make it optional.

### Is a form better than showing my email address?
Offer both. Forms give structure; some people prefer to write directly.

=== website-animations-performance
title: Website Animations: When They Help, When They Hurt
desc: How to use animation on a business website to delight visitors and guide attention without slowing the site, harming SEO or excluding users.
cat: design
kw: website animations, web animation performance, scroll animations seo, gsap animations website
takeaways: Animation should guide attention and add delight, never block content | Animate transform and opacity for smooth performance | Load animation code after the page is usable and respect reduced-motion settings | Content must exist in the HTML so search engines and AI can read it
---
Animation is what makes a website feel alive: a headline that rises into place, a gallery that glides, a button that responds to your cursor. It's also what makes bad websites unbearable. The difference is purpose and craft.

We use a lot of motion on this site. Here's how to do it without sacrificing speed, SEO or accessibility.

## What animation is good for

- **Guiding attention** to what matters
- **Showing relationships:** this panel came from that button
- **Giving feedback:** hover, press, success
- **Telling a story** as the visitor scrolls
- **Personality:** a brand that feels considered and premium

## When it hurts

- It delays content ("wait for the intro to finish")
- It distracts from the message
- It makes pages stutter or load slowly
- It triggers motion sickness
- It hides content from search engines

If an animation doesn't help the visitor understand or enjoy the page, cut it.

## Types worth using

### Entrance reveals

Text and images that fade or slide in as they enter the viewport. Subtle and effective.

### Scroll-driven effects

Parallax, pinned sections and horizontal galleries tied to scroll position. Powerful for storytelling, in moderation.

### Micro-interactions

Buttons that react, links that underline, forms that confirm. Small touches that make a site feel responsive.

### Page transitions

Smooth changes between pages make a multi-page site feel like one experience.

### Ambient motion

Gentle background movement, such as a gradient or shader, that adds atmosphere without demanding attention.

## Performance rules

### Animate the cheap properties

`transform` and `opacity` are handled by the graphics processor and stay smooth. Animating width, height, margins or shadows forces the browser to recalculate layout and causes jank.

### Load motion last

The page should be readable and usable before any animation library loads. Defer scripts so they don't block rendering.

### Keep libraries lean

A capable animation library adds perhaps 40 to 60 KB compressed. That's fine. Stacking several libraries and plugins isn't.

### Don't animate everything

Reserve motion for key moments. A page where everything moves is exhausting and slow.

### Pause off-screen work

Canvas and video backgrounds should stop when they're not visible.

### Test on a mid-range phone

If it stutters there, simplify.

See [Core Web Vitals explained](/blog/core-web-vitals-explained/).

## SEO: content first, motion second

Search engines and many AI crawlers read your HTML. Some don't run JavaScript at all.

- **All text must be in the HTML,** not injected by scripts
- **Don't hide content permanently** waiting for an animation that may never fire
- **Provide fallbacks** if scripts fail
- **Avoid layout shift** from elements that move after loading

On this site, every heading, paragraph and link exists in plain HTML. Animation only changes how it arrives.

## Accessibility

- **Respect `prefers-reduced-motion`.** People can ask their device to minimise animation; honour it.
- **No flashing** more than three times a second
- **Avoid motion that hijacks scrolling** unpredictably
- **Keep focus visible** for keyboard users

See [web accessibility basics](/blog/website-accessibility-basics/).

## Loading screens

A preloader can set the tone for a brand. Keep it brief (about a second), show it only on the first visit, and never use it to hide a slow site.

## Video

Background video is a form of animation with a heavy cost.

- Compress hard and keep it short
- Use a poster image
- Mute and loop
- Skip it on slow connections

More in [wedding videographer websites](/blog/wedding-videographer-website/).

| --- | --- |
| Hero | One signature moment |
| Sections | Simple reveal on entry |
| Interactive elements | Quick, subtle feedback |
| Scroll storytelling | One or two set pieces per page |
| Everything else | Still |

## Frequently asked questions

### Do animations hurt SEO?
Not when content is in the HTML, scripts are deferred and performance stays good.

### Are animations worth it for a small business?
A few well-chosen ones make a site feel premium and memorable. They should never get in the way of information.

### Which tools are used for web animation?
CSS for simple effects; libraries such as GSAP for complex, scroll-driven sequences; and smooth-scroll libraries for a polished feel.
