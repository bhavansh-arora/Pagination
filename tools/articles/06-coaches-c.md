Wellness coaching covers a lot of ground: health, nutrition, stress, sleep, hormones, burnout, habits. What unites the clients is that they've often tried things before and feel discouraged. Your website's job is to make them feel understood, show them a believable path and make starting easy.

## Set the tone

Wellness sites work best when they feel like a deep breath:

- **Colour:** soft, natural palettes with one confident accent
- **Photography:** real, warm, natural light; you, not stock models with salads
- **Space:** generous margins and short paragraphs
- **Language:** kind, clear and free of guilt

Calm doesn't mean vague. The booking button should still be the most obvious thing on the page.

## Say who you help

"I help people feel better" won't land. Specific does:

- "Coaching for women navigating perimenopause"
- "Burnout recovery for healthcare professionals"
- "Helping impact-driven women quiet self-doubt"

The narrower the statement, the more the right person feels seen. See [positioning your coaching niche](/blog/coaching-niche-positioning/).

## Make your method visible

Clients want to know there's a plan. Name your approach and show the steps. Lasting Change Wellness does this with the S.H.I.N.E. method: a simple acronym that turns an abstract service into a journey people can picture.

[[work:lastingchangewellness,co-allies]]

## Pages to include

- **Home:** who you help, the change you offer, proof and a call to action
- **About:** your own wellness story and credentials
- **Programmes:** clear options with what's included
- **Success stories**
- **Resources or blog:** recipes, guides, articles
- **Book a call**

Optional: a quiz ("What's your stress type?"), a free challenge, an online course area.

## Be careful with claims

Health is regulated, and trust is fragile. Protect both:

- Don't promise to diagnose, treat or cure
- Use phrases like "support", "guide" and "work towards"
- Add a short disclaimer that coaching isn't a substitute for medical care
- Be clear about your qualifications and scope
- Only use testimonials that reflect typical, honest experiences

## Credentials that matter

Display relevant ones: NBHWC board certification, health coaching diplomas, nutrition qualifications, a clinical background if you have one. A former psychologist turned wellness coach, for example, should say so prominently.

## Lead magnets that fit wellness

- A seven-day reset plan
- An energy or symptom tracker
- A simple meal-planning template
- A guided breathing audio
- A self-assessment quiz

More ideas in [20 lead magnets for coaches](/blog/lead-magnets-for-coaches/).

## Booking and intake

Offer a free introductory call with an embedded calendar. For wellness, a short intake question helps: "What would you most like to feel different three months from now?" Keep anything medical for a secure form after they've become a client.

## Selling programmes online

If you sell a group programme or course, your site can take payment directly through Stripe or connect to a course platform. Make the path short: programme page, pay, welcome email with next steps.

## SEO for wellness coaches

People search for help with specific struggles: "burnout coach", "health coach for PCOS", "stress management coaching". Build a page for each area you specialise in and write articles answering the questions clients ask. See [SEO for coaches](/blog/seo-for-coaches/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I show prices on a wellness coaching site?
A starting price helps set expectations. Many wellness coaches share full pricing for programmes and discuss bespoke options on a call.

### Can I sell supplements or products on my site?
Yes, with an online shop, but be careful about claims and check local regulations.

### Do I need a blog?
It helps with SEO and gives you content for social. Recipes, practical guides and myth-busting articles tend to do well.

Career coaching clients are usually motivated and measurable. They want a new role, a promotion, a pivot or the confidence to ask for more. That makes your website easier to write than most coaching sites, as long as you're specific.

## Lead with outcomes

Career coaching has something most coaching doesn't: clear results. Use them.

- "Clients typically land a new role within four months"
- "From senior engineer to engineering manager in one promotion cycle"
- Logos of companies clients have moved to (with permission)

Only publish numbers you can back up.

## Pick a niche

Career coaching is crowded. Niches cut through:

| Niche by | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Industry | Tech, healthcare, law, finance |
| Stage | Graduates, mid-career, executives |
| Transition | Career change, return to work, redundancy |
| Identity | Women in leadership, international professionals |

Live in Flow Coaching, for example, focuses on career transitions for tech professionals, and every line of the site speaks to that audience.

## What to put on the homepage

1. **Headline:** the feeling and the outcome. "Feeling stuck? Find your flow."
2. **Who you help:** in one line.
3. **How it works:** three steps.
4. **Results:** testimonials with role changes.
5. **Your credentials:** ICF level, recruiting or HR background, industries you know.
6. **Call to action:** a free 20 to 30 minute call.

## Services to consider listing

- One-to-one career coaching programmes
- CV and LinkedIn review
- Interview preparation
- Salary negotiation coaching
- Career change discovery
- Corporate outplacement

Give each its own section or page. Smaller, fixed-price services (like a CV review) are good entry points to longer programmes.

## Match your LinkedIn

Most career coaching clients find you on LinkedIn and click through. Your headline, photo and message should be consistent across both. Link your site's booking page in your LinkedIn featured section.

## Content that attracts career clients

Write the articles your clients are searching for:

- "How to change careers at 40"
- "Signs you've outgrown your job"
- "How to answer 'tell me about yourself'"
- "How to negotiate salary without losing the offer"

Each ranks on Google, shares well on LinkedIn and ends with an invitation to book. See [how to write a blog post that ranks](/blog/how-to-write-a-blog-post-that-ranks/).

## Lead magnets that work

- CV or LinkedIn headline templates
- A "stay or go" decision worksheet
- Interview question bank
- Salary negotiation scripts
- A career values quiz

## The booking path

Embed a calendar, ask two questions (current role and what they want to change), and send automatic reminders. The full approach is in [the discovery call funnel](/blog/discovery-call-booking-funnel/).

## B2B: outplacement and corporate programmes

If you work with employers, add a separate page for organisations with case studies, the outcomes you measure and an enquiry form for HR. Corporate buyers need different proof from individual clients.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should career coaches guarantee results?
No. You can share typical outcomes and client stories, but a job offer depends on factors outside your control.

### Do I need separate pages for each service?
Yes, if you want each to rank on Google and to give prospects enough detail to choose.

### How do career coaches get clients online?
A mix of LinkedIn content, search-optimised articles, referrals and a website that converts visitors into discovery calls.

"I help people live their best life" is true of every coach and persuasive to no one. A niche isn't a cage; it's a signpost. It tells the right person, in a second, "this is for you".

## Why niching works

- **Recognition.** People respond to messages that describe their exact situation.
- **Referrals.** "She's the coach for new managers in tech" is easy to remember and pass on.
- **Search.** "Career coach for nurses" is far easier to rank for than "life coach".
- **Pricing.** Specialists charge more than generalists.
- **Content.** You always know what to write about.

## The three-part positioning formula

**I help [who] to [outcome] without/despite [problem].**

- "I help first-time founders lead growing teams without burning out."
- "I help women navigating fertility treatment feel supported and in control."
- "I help tech professionals move into work that feels like flow."

Write ten versions. Read each aloud. The right one feels slightly scary because it's specific.

## Four ways to niche

| Dimension | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Who | Lawyers, new mothers, expats, creatives |
| Problem | Burnout, imposter feelings, divorce, redundancy |
| Outcome | Promotion, career change, confidence, boundaries |
| Method | Somatic work, strengths-based, a named framework |

The strongest niches combine two: a who and a problem.

## Put it on your website

### The hero

Your positioning belongs in your headline or the line directly beneath it. Don't make visitors hunt for it.

### The "is this you?" section

Three or four bullet points describing your ideal client's situation in their own words.

### Your programmes

Name and describe them in terms of the niche outcome.

### Your testimonials

Choose stories from clients who fit the niche. People look for someone like themselves.

### Your content

Articles, lead magnets and social posts all speak to the same person.

George Dupont Leadership targets sports franchises and C-suite teams with a trademarked system. Co-Allies speaks to women on the path to motherhood and leaders in transition. Different audiences, same clarity.

## "But I don't want to turn clients away"

You won't. Niching is about marketing focus, not a rule about who you can work with. People outside your niche will still find you through referrals and often say "I know I'm not exactly your usual client, but...". What changes is that your ideal clients finally recognise you.

## How to find your niche

1. **Look at your best clients.** Who did you love working with and get great results for? What did they have in common?
2. **Look at your own story.** Many coaches serve a past version of themselves.
3. **Check demand.** Are people searching for it and paying for it?
4. **Test.** Change your headline and LinkedIn bio for 90 days and see who shows up.

## Signs your positioning is working

- Discovery calls start with "I read your site and it felt like you were describing me"
- Referrals arrive pre-sold
- You stop competing on price
- Writing content gets easier

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I have more than one niche?
You can serve two closely related audiences, each with its own page. More than that and the homepage message blurs.

### What if I pick the wrong niche?
You adjust. Updating a headline and a few pages is quick. Staying vague costs more than choosing imperfectly.

### Do I need to niche from day one?
No. Many coaches discover their niche after their first 20 clients. Start reasonably specific and narrow as patterns emerge.

=== coach-website-copywriting
title: Coaching Website Copywriting: Headlines and Words That Convert

cat: coaches
kw: coaching website copywriting, coaching website headlines, copywriting for coaches, coaching website copy
takeaways: Write to one person in the words they'd use themselves | Your headline should name their problem or their goal | Cut jargon like "empower" and "unlock your potential" | Every section should end by pointing to the next step
---
Design gets attention. Words get the booking. Most coaching websites lose people in the first sentence by talking about the coach when the visitor is thinking about themselves.

Here's how to write copy that sounds like you and speaks to them.

## Rule one: write to one person

Picture a real client, ideally your favourite one. Write as if you're sending them a message. Use "you" twice as often as "I".

## Rule two: use their words

Go through discovery call notes, emails and testimonials. Collect the exact phrases clients use: "I feel like I'm treading water", "I know I'm capable of more". Those phrases are your copy. They'll outperform anything you invent.

## Headline formulas that work

**Problem then promise**
"Feeling stuck? Find your flow."

**The outcome**
"Clear, confident and in control again."

**The transformation**
"Transform life's hardest moments into your greatest comeback."

**The enemy**
"Cultures that perform under pressure. Without burnout, blame or busy nonsense."

**The identity**
"For women who are done wishing and ready to act."

Notice what none of them say: "Welcome to my website" or "Certified Professional Coach".

[[work:sharontabak,highfifteen]]

## Section by section

### Subheadline

One sentence: who you help and how. "One-to-one coaching for tech professionals ready for a career that fits."

### The problem section

Describe their situation so accurately they feel understood. Three short lines beat one long paragraph.

### The solution section

Introduce your coaching as the bridge. Focus on what changes for them.

### How it works

Three steps. Short verbs: Book. Plan. Grow.

### About snippet

Two or three sentences on why you, with a link to your [about page](/blog/coach-about-page/).

### Call to action

Specific and low-pressure: "Book your free 30-minute call". Add a reassurance: "No pressure. Just a conversation."

## Words to cut

| Instead of | Try |
| --- | --- |
| Empower you to unlock your potential | Help you [specific outcome] |
| Holistic, transformative journey | A three-month programme |
| I am passionate about | (just say what you do) |
| Solutions | (name the actual thing) |
| Leverage, synergy, optimise | Use, together, improve |

If a phrase could appear on any coach's site, it isn't doing any work on yours.

## Make it scannable

Most visitors skim first and read second.

- Short paragraphs (one to three lines)
- A heading every few paragraphs
- Bullet points for lists
- Bold for the key phrase in a section, sparingly

[[cta]]

## Calls to action that get clicked

- Use the same wording everywhere so it becomes familiar
- Start with a verb: Book, Start, Get
- Say what happens: "Book a free 30-minute call"
- Place one after every major section

For more examples, see [call-to-action examples that convert](/blog/call-to-action-examples/).

## The read-aloud test

Read every page out loud. Anywhere you stumble, sound stiff or wouldn't say it to a client's face, rewrite. This catches most problems.

## Should you use AI to write your copy?

AI tools are useful for outlining and tidying, but they default to the same generic phrases every other coach's site uses. Use them to speed up a draft built from your own client language, then edit until it sounds like you.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should my homepage be?
Long enough to answer the visitor's main questions and build trust; usually 500 to 900 words broken into clear sections.

### Should I write in first person?
Yes, for a solo coach. "I" and "you" feel personal. Use "we" only if you're genuinely a team.
