Solar is one of the biggest purchases a homeowner makes after the house itself. They don't decide in an afternoon. They research, compare installers, read reviews and run numbers, often over several weeks. The installer whose website answers their questions best is usually the one who gets the call.

We've built solar websites for installers in Ireland, Canada, the United States and New Zealand. This is the structure that works.

## What a solar website has to do

1. **Explain** what's possible and what it costs, in plain language
2. **Prove** you're certified, experienced and local
3. **Personalise** the numbers to the visitor's home
4. **Capture** the lead when they're ready

Miss any one and visitors move on to a competitor's site that covers it.

## The homepage

### Above the fold

- A headline about the benefit: "Go solar. Save big." or "Powering Ireland's solar future."
- A line on who you serve and where
- A primary button: "Get my free quote"
- A trust strip: years in business, installs completed, review rating, key certifications

### Further down

- Services at a glance: solar PV, batteries, EV charging
- How it works in three or four steps
- A savings calculator or estimator
- Incentives and grants available
- Recent installations
- Reviews
- FAQ
- A final quote form

## The two features that convert

### A savings calculator

Homeowners want to know one thing: what will *I* save? A simple calculator that takes a postcode or ZIP and a monthly bill, then estimates system size, savings and payback, turns a generic pitch into a personal one. It also captures a lead at the perfect moment. More in [should your solar website have a savings calculator?](/blog/solar-savings-calculator/)

### A short quote form

Three to five fields to start, more on a second step. Long forms kill conversions. See [designing a solar quote form that converts](/blog/solar-quote-form/).

## Trust signals

Solar has a reputation problem in some markets: pushy sales, vanished installers, broken promises. Counter it visibly:

- Accreditation logos (SEAI, NABCEP, CEC, MCS or your local body)
- Manufacturer partnerships and warranty terms
- Years trading and number of installs
- Real photos of your team and vans
- Google reviews with a live count
- Local address and phone number

Full list in [trust signals every solar website needs](/blog/solar-website-trust-signals/).

## Design for clarity

- **Clean and bright,** or premium and dark; either works if text is readable
- **Real project photos** over stock
- **Simple diagrams** for how systems work
- **Numbers shown clearly:** savings, payback, warranty years
- **Mobile first:** many visitors arrive from social ads on their phones

## Get found locally

Solar is a local business. People search "solar installers [town]" and "solar panels [county]". You need:

- Location in titles and headings
- Genuine service area pages
- A complete Google Business Profile with reviews
- Local schema markup

Our guide to [SEO for solar companies](/blog/solar-seo/) covers this in depth.

## Follow up fast

A website lead is worth most in the first hour. Send an instant confirmation, route the enquiry to your CRM, and call or text the same day. The best website in the world can't rescue a lead that waits three days.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does a solar company website cost?
A lead-generation site with a calculator and quote flow typically costs $4,000 to $10,000, depending on pages and integrations.

### Do I need a calculator?
It isn't essential, but it's one of the strongest lead-capture tools available to solar installers.

### How long does it take to build?
Usually three to five weeks, including the calculator and CRM integration.

Every solar visitor arrives with the same question: *what would I save?* Most websites answer with "book a free consultation to find out". A calculator answers on the spot, and that changes the whole conversation.

## Why calculators work

- **Personal.** A number for their home beats a national average.
- **Instant.** No waiting for a callback.
- **Engaging.** Interacting with a tool keeps people on the page.
- **Qualifying.** You learn their bill, location and rough system size before you call.
- **Lead capture.** "Email me my full savings report" is an easy yes.

## What to ask

Keep the inputs minimal:

1. **Postcode or ZIP** (for sunlight hours and electricity rates)
2. **Average monthly electricity bill**

Optional refinements:

3. Roof direction or type
4. Whether they have or want an EV
5. Interest in a battery

Two inputs get the most completions. Offer the rest as "refine my estimate".

## What to show

- **Recommended system size** (kW and approximate panel count)
- **Estimated annual savings**
- **Estimated payback period**
- **Lifetime savings** over 25 years
- **CO₂ avoided,** translated into something relatable such as trees or car miles
- **Available incentives**

Show ranges rather than single figures. "$1,400 to $1,900 a year" is more believable than "$1,652".

## The lead capture moment

There are two approaches:

**Gate the result:** ask for contact details before showing anything. Captures more data but frustrates visitors.

**Show first, then offer more:** display the estimate, then offer a detailed report, a roof assessment or a firm quote in exchange for details. This builds goodwill and still converts strongly.

We recommend the second.

## Make the numbers trustworthy

- State assumptions: electricity price, export rate, panel output, degradation
- Link to your data sources
- Label results as estimates
- Don't inflate savings; a sales call that disappoints kills the deal
- Update rates and incentives regularly

## How it works under the hood

A basic calculator needs:

- Average solar irradiance by region
- Typical electricity tariffs
- System cost per kW
- Current incentive amounts

These can sit in a simple data table that your team updates. More advanced versions pull live data from mapping and solar APIs to assess the actual roof.

## Design tips

- Put it high on the homepage and on its own page
- Use sliders and large tap targets
- Animate the result so it feels rewarding
- Keep it fast; no heavy third-party widgets
- Follow the result with one clear button: "Get my exact quote"

## Is it worth the build cost?

A custom calculator typically adds $1,000 to $3,000 to a website project. If it generates even a few extra qualified leads a month, it pays for itself with the first installation.

## Alternatives if you're not ready

## Frequently asked questions

### How accurate do the estimates need to be?
Accurate enough to be honest and consistent with what your surveyor will say. Ranges and clear assumptions protect you.

### Can the calculator feed my CRM?
Yes. Submissions can go straight to HubSpot, GoHighLevel, Pipedrive, a spreadsheet or your inbox.

### Should I use a third-party calculator widget?
They're quick to add but often slow, generic and branded by someone else. A custom calculator matches your site and keeps the lead data yours.
