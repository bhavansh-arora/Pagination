"How much does a website cost?" is the first question almost every business owner asks us, and the honest answer is a range. A website is closer to a renovation than a product on a shelf: the price depends on what you're building, who builds it and how much of the thinking is done for you.

This guide gives you real numbers, explains what actually moves the price, and shows you how to budget without getting burned.

## The short answer

Here are the typical ranges we see across the market in 2026 for a small business website:

| Route | Typical upfront cost | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| DIY builder (Wix, Squarespace) | $0 to $500, plus your time | Brand-new businesses testing an idea |
| Freelancer using a template | $500 to $2,500 | Simple brochure sites on a tight budget |
| Custom design by a small studio | $2,500 to $10,000 | Established businesses that need leads |
| Agency build with strategy and content | $10,000 to $50,000+ | Larger companies with complex needs |

Most established small businesses (a coaching practice, a salon, a solar installer, a wedding photographer) land in the third row. That's the range where you get custom design, proper SEO foundations and someone thinking about conversion, without paying for a room full of account managers.

## What actually drives the cost

Page count is the thing people fixate on, but it's rarely the biggest factor. These are.

### 1. Custom design vs a template

A template gives you someone else's layout with your logo dropped in. Custom design starts from your customers: what they need to see, in what order, to trust you and get in touch. Custom takes more hours, which is why it costs more, but it's also why it tends to convert better.

### 2. Functionality

A five-page site that only displays information is simple. The cost rises when the site has to *do* things:

- Online booking or appointment requests
- Payments, deposits or a full online store
- Calculators, quizzes or quote builders
- Member areas, courses or client portals
- Integrations with your CRM, calendar or email platform

Each of these is a mini software project. A solar savings calculator, for example, can take longer to build than the rest of the site combined.

### 3. Content

Someone has to write the words and source the images. If you supply polished copy and professional photos, the build is faster. If your designer is also writing your copy, structuring your offers and directing photography, expect to pay for that time. It's usually worth it: content is what persuades.

### 4. SEO depth

Every decent build should include the basics: fast pages, clean structure, title tags, a sitemap. Deeper work costs extra: keyword research, location pages, schema markup, and migrating an old site without losing rankings. If you rely on Google for leads, read our [technical SEO checklist](/blog/technical-seo-checklist/) before you sign anything.

### 5. Who you hire

A freelancer has low overheads. An agency has project managers, strategists and an office. A small founder-led studio sits in between: senior work without the layers. Rates also vary a lot by country, which is why a studio like ours can deliver agency-level work for clients in the US, UK and Australia at a lower price than a local agency.

## The ongoing costs people forget

The build is a one-off. These are not:

- **Domain name:** $10 to $20 a year for most `.com` names.
- **Hosting:** $5 to $50 a month for a typical small business site. Builders like Wix and Squarespace bundle this into a $16 to $40 monthly plan.
- **Maintenance:** $0 to $150 a month depending on the platform. WordPress sites need regular plugin and security updates. Hand-coded static sites need very little.
- **Email:** around $6 to $12 per user per month for Google Workspace or Microsoft 365.
- **Tools:** booking software, email marketing and CRM subscriptions, if you use them.

A fair all-in running cost for most small businesses is $20 to $100 a month. If you're being quoted $300 a month for "hosting" on a five-page site, ask exactly what's included. We cover this in detail in our [website maintenance guide](/blog/website-maintenance-guide/).

## Three sample budgets

To make this concrete, here's how three common projects typically price out.

**A one-page site for a new coach.** A single long page with an about section, services, testimonials and a booking link. Expect $800 to $2,500 depending on design polish and whether copy is included.

**A lead-generation site for a local service business.** Eight to fifteen pages: home, about, a page per service, a few location pages, reviews, contact and a quote form. Expect $3,000 to $8,000 with proper SEO setup.

**A booking or e-commerce site.** A salon with online booking and deposits, or a store with 50 products and Stripe checkout. Expect $5,000 to $15,000 depending on the catalog and integrations.

## How to avoid overpaying

1. **Get a fixed quote, in writing.** Hourly billing puts all the risk on you. A fixed price for a defined scope puts it where it belongs.
2. **Ask what you'll own.** You should get the domain, the code, the design files and admin access. If the answer is vague, walk away. More on this in [do you really own your website?](/blog/website-ownership-explained/)
3. **Check live work, not mockups.** Anyone can make a pretty picture. Click through real sites they've launched and test them on your phone.
4. **Separate must-haves from nice-to-haves.** Launch with what you need to win customers, then add features once the site is paying for itself.
5. **See something before you pay.** A studio confident in its work will show you a direction first. That's why we offer a [free website preview](/free-website-preview/): we design your homepage on a live link before you commit to anything.

## Is a cheap website a false economy?

Sometimes. A $300 website that brings in no enquiries costs more than a $5,000 website that brings in two new clients a month. The right question isn't "what's the cheapest site I can get?" but "what will this site be worth to my business?"

If one new client is worth $2,000 to you, a site that converts even one extra visitor a month pays for itself quickly. That's the lens to use.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I build a website for free?
Yes. Builders like Wix and Google Sites have free plans, but they show ads, use a subdomain and limit features. They're fine for testing an idea, not for a business that wants to look established.

### Why do quotes for the same website vary so much?
Because the scope behind them differs. One quote may be a template with your logo; another includes strategy, copywriting, custom design and SEO. Ask each provider to list exactly what's included and compare like for like.

### Should I pay monthly or upfront?
Upfront builds usually cost less over three years and leave you owning the site. "Website as a service" monthly plans lower the barrier but often mean you're renting, and lose the site if you stop paying. Check the contract.

## When a builder is the right choice

A builder makes sense if:

- **You're validating a new business.** Don't spend $5,000 on a website for an idea you haven't sold yet.
- **Your budget is under $1,000.** A good template beats a bad custom build.
- **You enjoy doing it yourself** and have the time to learn design, copy and SEO basics.
- **Your site is a simple brochure** and most of your customers come from referrals anyway.

Squarespace in particular produces clean, respectable sites, and for a lot of new businesses that's enough for year one.

## Where builders start to hurt

### Everyone looks the same

Templates are popular for a reason, which means your competitors are using them too. When a customer has three tabs open and all three sites share the same layout, the cheapest price tends to win. Distinctive design is how you avoid competing on price.

### Speed and Core Web Vitals

Builders load their entire platform on every page, whether you use the features or not. That extra weight shows up in load times, especially on mobile. A hand-coded site only ships what the page needs. Speed affects both rankings and conversion; we explain how in [Core Web Vitals explained](/blog/core-web-vitals-explained/).

### The feature ceiling

Builders are great until you want something they don't offer: a savings calculator, a multi-step quote form, a custom booking flow, a page layout the template doesn't support. Then you're stacking third-party widgets, each with its own monthly fee and its own look.

### SEO depth

Modern builders handle the basics well. Where they fall short is fine control: custom schema markup, clean URL structures, precise control over what loads and when. For competitive local searches, those details add up. See our [schema markup guide](/blog/schema-markup-guide/) for what you'd be missing.

### You're renting

On a builder, your site lives on their platform under their terms. If they raise prices or retire a feature, you adapt. You can't take a Wix site and host it elsewhere. A custom site is a set of files you own outright.

## The hidden cost of DIY: your time

A builder's sticker price ignores the biggest cost. Business owners typically spend 30 to 60 hours building their own site, then more hours every month fiddling with it. If your time is worth $75 an hour, that "free" website cost $3,000 before you count the leads a generic site didn't convert.

The question isn't whether you *can* build it yourself. It's whether that's the best use of your week.

## A middle path

It doesn't have to be all or nothing. Three options sit between DIY and fully bespoke:

1. **Start on a builder, upgrade later.** Totally valid. Just make sure you own your domain so the move is painless.
2. **Buy a ready-made custom site.** A pre-built, professionally designed site that you rebrand. We sell a small number of these on our [templates page](/templates/); each sells to one owner only.
3. **Custom design, simple build.** A focused one-page custom site costs far less than a full build and still looks nothing like a template.

## How to decide

Ask yourself three questions:

1. **Is the website a sales channel or a business card?** If customers find and choose you through it, invest. If it just confirms you exist, a builder is fine.
2. **What's one new customer worth?** If the answer is more than a few hundred dollars, a better-converting site pays for itself quickly.
3. **Do you want to spend your evenings on this?** Be honest.

If you're on the fence, the lowest-risk way to decide is to see what custom would look like for your business. Our [free website preview](/free-website-preview/) exists for exactly this: we design your homepage on a live link, you compare it with what you have, and you decide with real evidence.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Wix or Squarespace bad for SEO?
No. Both cover the fundamentals and plenty of builder sites rank well. They offer less control over speed and advanced technical SEO, which matters more in competitive markets.

### Can I move from Squarespace to a custom site later?
Yes. Your content can be migrated and your URLs redirected so you keep your rankings. Read [how to redesign without losing SEO](/blog/seo-migration-redesign/) before you switch.

### Is WordPress a builder or custom?
Both, depending on how it's used. A WordPress site with a bought theme and 30 plugins behaves like a builder. A custom-coded WordPress theme is closer to a custom site, with more maintenance than a static build.

The honest answer: anywhere from a few days to a few months. A one-page site for a coach is a very different project from a 200-product online store. What matters more than the average is understanding which parts take time, and which of those you control.

## Typical timelines by project type

| Project | Typical timeline |
| --- | --- |
| One-page site or landing page | 3 to 7 days |
| Small business site (5 to 10 pages) | 1 to 3 weeks |
| Larger site with location and service pages (15 to 40 pages) | 3 to 6 weeks |
| Booking site with payments | 3 to 6 weeks |
| E-commerce store | 4 to 10 weeks |
| Web app or custom platform | 2 to 6 months |

These assume an experienced team and a client who responds within a day or two. Stretch either and the numbers stretch with them.

## The four phases of a website project

### 1. Discovery (1 to 3 days)

A conversation about your business, customers, competitors and goals. The output is an agreed scope: which pages, which features, what success looks like. Skipping this phase is the fastest way to make a project slow.

### 2. Design (3 to 7 days)

The homepage usually comes first because it sets the visual language for everything else. Once that direction is approved, inner pages follow quickly. At Code Bunny this is where our [free website preview](/free-website-preview/) fits: we design your homepage on a live link before you've committed, so the riskiest decision is made early.

### 3. Build (1 to 3 weeks)

Design becomes working pages: responsive layouts, forms, animations, integrations, SEO setup. For a brochure site this is fast. Features like booking, payments and calculators are where build time grows.

### 4. Launch (2 to 4 days)

Testing on real devices, connecting the domain, setting up analytics and Search Console, redirecting old URLs and submitting the sitemap. It's tempting to rush this. Don't: a broken form on launch day costs real enquiries.

## What actually causes delays

In our experience across more than a hundred projects, the code is almost never what holds things up. These are the real culprits.

### Waiting for content

This is the big one. The design is done, the pages are built, and everyone waits three weeks for the about page copy. If you take one thing from this article: **start gathering your content before the project starts.** That means:

- A short description of each service
- Your story and credentials
- Testimonials and reviews
- Logo files and brand colours
- Photos of you, your team and your work
- Logins for your domain and any existing tools

If writing isn't your strength, ask your designer to draft the copy for you to edit. Reacting to a draft is far quicker than staring at a blank page.

### Too many decision-makers

Feedback from one person takes a day. Feedback from a committee takes a week and often contradicts itself. Nominate one person to collect opinions and give a single, consolidated response.

### Scope creep

"Can we also add a blog, a members area and a quiz?" Each addition is reasonable. Together they double the project. Park new ideas in a phase-two list and launch what was agreed.

### Slow feedback loops

A two-week project has room for two or three review rounds. If each one takes five days to come back, it's now a six-week project.

## How to launch faster

1. **Write a brief.** Even one page helps. Use our [website brief template](/blog/website-brief-template/).
2. **Collect content first.** See the list above.
3. **Share examples.** Three sites you like and one you don't saves days of guessing.
4. **Agree the scope and freeze it.** New ideas go on the phase-two list.
5. **Give feedback in one batch, within 48 hours.**
6. **Launch, then improve.** A good site that's live beats a perfect site that isn't. You can refine copy and add pages after launch.

## Can a website really be built in a week?

Yes, when the scope is tight and the content is ready. We regularly take a business from first call to live site in under two weeks. The projects that manage it share the same traits: a clear offer, a decisive owner and content supplied up front.

What can't be rushed is the thinking. A site built quickly on a muddled message will underperform a site built quickly on a clear one. If you're not sure what your site should say, start with [what pages a small business website should have](/blog/what-pages-should-a-small-business-website-have/).

## How long until the website brings in leads?

Launch day isn't the finish line. Visitors from ads and social media can arrive immediately. Search traffic takes longer: Google needs to crawl, index and trust a new site, which typically takes a few weeks for brand searches and three to six months for competitive terms. We break this down in [how long does SEO take?](/blog/how-long-does-seo-take/)

## Frequently asked questions

### Why do some agencies quote three months for a simple site?
Often it's process overhead: multiple teams, scheduled review meetings and a queue of other projects. A small senior team can move faster because the person you speak to is the person doing the work.

### Does a faster build mean lower quality?
Not necessarily. Speed comes from experience, reusable components and clear decisions. Quality drops when testing is skipped or content is rushed, so make sure those aren't what's being cut.

### What do you need from me to get started?
A short description of your business and customers, any existing website or social profiles, your logo if you have one, and a few sites you like. That's enough for us to design a first preview.

Websites rarely fail loudly. They just quietly stop pulling their weight: fewer enquiries, more "I couldn't find your prices" phone calls, an awkward pause when someone asks for your web address.

Here are twelve signs it's time for a change, roughly in order of how much they cost you.

## 1. It doesn't bring in enquiries

The clearest sign of all. If people visit and leave without calling, booking or filling in a form, the site isn't doing its job. Check your analytics: if you get 500 visitors a month and two enquiries, the problem isn't traffic.

## 2. It's slow on a phone

More than half of web traffic is mobile, and visitors won't wait. Test your site at [PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev/). If the mobile score is in the red or the page takes more than three seconds to become usable, you're losing people before they've read a word. See [Core Web Vitals explained](/blog/core-web-vitals-explained/) for what the numbers mean.

## 3. It looks dated

Design trends move, and visitors judge credibility in a fraction of a second. Tiny text, stock photos of handshakes, a cluttered sidebar and a copyright date from years ago all say the same thing: this business isn't paying attention.

## 4. It's awkward to use on mobile

Being technically "responsive" isn't enough. Can you tap the phone number? Are the buttons big enough for a thumb? Does the menu work? Is the text readable without zooming? Borrow a friend's phone and try to book your own service.

## 5. You're embarrassed to share it

If you hesitate before giving out your URL, or apologise for the site when you do, trust that instinct. Your website should be something you're proud to put on a business card.

## 6. You can't update it yourself

If changing your opening hours means emailing a developer and waiting a week, the site will always be out of date. A modern site should let you edit everyday content yourself. When we launched Dan Reynolds Photography, it shipped with a built-in editor and blog so Dan can publish his own stories.

## 7. It doesn't show up on Google

Search your main service plus your town. If you're nowhere on the first page (and competitors with worse reviews are), the site likely has thin content, weak structure or technical problems. Our [local SEO guide](/blog/local-seo-guide/) explains what Google looks for.

[[audit]]

## 8. Your business has changed

New services, new prices, a new location, a new audience. If the website describes the business you ran three years ago, it's attracting the wrong customers and under-selling the right ones.

## 9. Visitors leave immediately

A high bounce rate on your homepage usually means a mismatch: people expected one thing and found another, or couldn't tell within five seconds what you do. A clear headline, a strong image and one obvious next step fix most of it. See [homepage design best practices](/blog/homepage-design-best-practices/).

## 10. It isn't secure

No padlock in the address bar (HTTPS) means browsers show a "Not secure" warning. That alone will stop people filling in your contact form. Outdated plugins are a related risk: they're the most common way small business sites get hacked.

## 11. Your competitors' sites are better

Customers compare. Open your top three competitors side by side with your own site. If theirs are clearer, faster and more convincing, that's where the enquiries are going.

## 12. It's built on something nobody supports

A discontinued builder, a theme that hasn't been updated since 2019, or code written by a developer who's disappeared. If nobody can safely change the site, it's a liability.

## Redesign, rebuild or repair?

Not every problem needs a new website.

**Repair** when the design is fine but specific things are broken: slow images, a missing call to action, outdated copy. These are often a few days' work.

**Redesign** when the structure and platform are sound but the look and messaging are tired.

**Rebuild** when the platform itself is the problem: it's slow, insecure, impossible to edit or can't support what you need.

If you ticked four or more signs on this list, a rebuild is usually cheaper in the long run than patching.

## What a good redesign protects

A new site should never cost you the traffic you already have. Before anything is switched off:

- Every old URL should redirect to its new equivalent
- Page titles and content that rank well should be kept or improved
- Analytics and Search Console should stay connected
- The new site should be tested on real phones

### How often should a business redesign its website?
Most businesses benefit from a significant refresh every three to five years, with smaller updates in between. If the site still converts well and loads fast, there's no need to change it just because time has passed.

### Will a redesign hurt my SEO?
It can if it's done carelessly. With proper redirects and preserved content, a redesign normally improves rankings because the new site is faster and better structured.
