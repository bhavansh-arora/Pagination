takeaways: Start with three to five easy fields and ask the rest on step two | Multi-step forms with a progress bar outperform one long page | Reassure at the point of submission: no obligation, no spam, fast response | Route every lead to your CRM and reply within minutes
---
A homeowner has read your page, likes what they see and clicks "Get a quote". Then they meet a 14-field form asking for their roof pitch and annual kilowatt-hours. Half of them leave.

Your quote form is the narrowest point of your funnel. Small improvements here are worth more than almost any other change on the site.

## The principle: easy first, detail later

Ask for the minimum needed to start a conversation, then gather detail once they've committed.

### Step 1: about the property

- Postcode or ZIP
- Property type: home, business, farm
- Average monthly electricity bill (a dropdown of ranges is easier than typing)

### Step 2: about what they want

- Interested in: solar, battery, EV charger (tick boxes)
- Roof type or a note that they're not sure
- Timeline: as soon as possible, within 3 months, just researching

### Step 3: contact details

- Name
- Phone
- Email
- Best time to call

By step three they've invested effort and are far more likely to finish.

## Why multi-step works

- Each screen looks short and achievable
- A progress bar creates momentum
- Easy questions first build commitment
- You can capture partial data from those who drop off

## What not to ask up front

- Annual usage in kWh (most people don't know)
- Roof pitch, orientation and dimensions (your survey will find out)
- A bill upload as a required field (offer it as optional)
- Full address before they trust you

## Trust cues beside the form

- "Free, no-obligation quote"
- "We'll never share your details"
- "Response within one business day"
- Accreditation logos
- A short review
- A photo of a real team member who'll call

[[cta]]

## Button wording

"Submit" is a dead word. Use what the visitor gets:

- "Get my free quote"
- "See my savings"
- "Check my eligibility"

## Mobile matters most

Many solar enquiries start on a phone, often from a Facebook ad.

- Large tap targets and tick boxes
- Number keyboards for phone and postcode fields
- Autofill enabled
- No tiny dropdowns

## After they submit

1. **Thank-you page** confirming what happens next and when
2. **Instant email or SMS** with the same message and a link to a case study
3. **CRM entry** created automatically with source tracking
4. **A call within minutes** if in business hours

Offer a way to book a call slot directly on the thank-you page; it saves phone tag.

## Offer alternatives

Not everyone likes forms. Next to it, offer:

- Click-to-call
- WhatsApp or SMS
- A callback request with a single phone field

## Test and improve

Track how many people start the form versus finish it. If lots drop at a particular step, simplify that step. Small changes, such as removing one field or rewording a button, can lift completions noticeably.

General principles are in [contact forms that convert](/blog/contact-form-best-practices/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I ask for a phone number?
Yes for solar, because a conversation is needed. Make it clear why: "so we can confirm a few details about your roof".

### Should the form be on every page?
A button to it should be. Put the full form on a dedicated quote page and at the bottom of key pages.

### Can the form integrate with our CRM?
Yes. Most CRMs accept web form data directly or through tools like Zapier.

=== solar-service-area-pages
title: Service Area Pages for Solar Installers (Without Thin Content)

takeaways: One strong page per priority area beats dozens of near-duplicates | Each page needs genuinely local content: projects, reviews, conditions and incentives | Link area pages to service pages and case studies | Start with the five to ten areas that matter most
---
If you install solar across twenty towns but your website only mentions your head office, you're competing for one town's searches. Service area pages fix that, when they're done properly. Done lazily (the same paragraph with the town name swapped) they can drag your whole site down.

## Why they work

Google tries to show local results for searches like "solar installers Galway". A page that's clearly about solar in Galway, with evidence you actually work there, is a strong match.

## What makes a page "thin"

- Identical text across pages with only the place name changed
- No local detail
- No unique images
- No reason for a human to read it

Google's guidelines call these doorway pages. Avoid them.

## The template for a strong area page

### 1. Headline and intro

"Solar Panel Installation in [Town]" plus two or three sentences on your experience there.

### 2. Local proof

- Number of systems installed in the area
- Two or three local projects with photos, system size and savings
- Reviews from customers in that town

### 3. Local conditions

- Typical sunlight hours and expected generation
- Common roof types and housing stock
- Local electricity provider and export arrangements
- Planning or permit notes specific to the council or county

### 4. Incentives

National schemes plus anything regional. Link to your full [incentives guide](/blog/solar-incentives-content/).

### 5. Services available

Short summaries linking to residential, commercial, battery and EV pages.

### 6. FAQs

Three or four questions specific to the area.

### 7. Call to action

A quote form with the town pre-filled.

Aim for 500 to 900 words that couldn't be pasted onto another town's page.

## How many pages?

Start with five to ten: your base and the largest towns or counties you serve. Add more as you complete projects there and have something real to say. Quality first.

## URL and linking structure

- `/areas/` index page listing every location
- `/areas/galway/` for each
- Link from each service page to the relevant area pages and back
- Link case studies to their area page

Clear structure helps both visitors and search engines. More in our [internal linking guide](/blog/internal-linking-guide/).

[[cta]]

## Titles and meta

- **Title:** "Solar Panel Installers in Galway | [Company]"
- **Meta description:** mention the area, a proof point and the quote offer

See [title tags and meta descriptions](/blog/title-tags-meta-descriptions/).

## Schema

Add LocalBusiness schema with `areaServed` listing your locations, plus FAQ schema on each area page. See our [schema markup guide](/blog/schema-markup-guide/).

## Keep them alive

Every time you complete an install in an area, add the photo and a line to that page. Fresh, real content keeps pages ranking and makes them more persuasive.

## Area pages and your Google Business Profile

Your profile's service areas should match the areas on your site. Consistency reinforces relevance. See the [Google Business Profile checklist](/blog/google-business-profile-optimization/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I create pages for towns where I haven't installed yet?
Yes, if you genuinely serve them and can write useful local content. Be honest about experience.

### Will many similar pages hurt my site?
Near-duplicates can. Unique, useful pages won't.

### Do I need a physical office in each area?
No. Service area pages are designed for businesses that travel to customers.

=== solar-website-trust-signals
title: Trust Signals Every Solar Website Needs
desc: The trust signals that reassure solar buyers, from accreditations and warranties to reviews and real project photos, and where to place them on your website.
cat: solar
kw: solar website trust signals, solar company credibility, solar installer accreditation, solar reviews
takeaways: Solar is a high-cost, long-term purchase, so buyers look hard for reasons to trust you | Accreditations, warranties and years in business belong near the top of the page | Real photos and local reviews beat stock imagery and generic claims | Transparency about price, process and aftercare is itself a trust signal
---
A solar system costs thousands and lasts 25 years. Buyers have heard stories about cowboy installers and companies that vanished before the warranty did. Before they'll hand over their details, they need evidence you're not one of them.

Here are the signals that matter, in rough order of impact.

## 1. Accreditations and certifications

Display the logos of the bodies that matter in your market: SEAI registration in Ireland, NABCEP in the US, CEC or SEANZ in Australasia, MCS in the UK, plus electrical licences. Link each to your listing where possible so visitors can verify.

## 2. Reviews, with numbers

"Rated 5/5 from 67 reviews" near the top of the page is powerful. Show the platform (Google, Trustpilot), the count and a few full reviews with names and locations.

## 3. Years in business and installs completed

"Solar PV specialists since 2015" and "1,200+ systems installed" answer the question "will you still be here?"

## 4. Warranties, spelled out

- Panel product and performance warranty
- Inverter warranty
- Battery warranty
- Your own workmanship warranty

Put the years in a simple table. Vague "industry-leading warranties" reassures nobody.

## 5. Manufacturer partnerships

Certified installer status with recognised brands shows you're trained and supported.

## 6. Real photos

Your team, your vans, your installs. Drone shots of finished roofs in recognisable local settings are ideal. Stock photos of smiling families signal the opposite of what you intend.

## 7. Case studies with real numbers

System size, cost, annual savings, payback, and a quote from the customer. Include commercial and farm projects if you do them.

## 8. A visible, local presence

Full address, local phone number, service area map, company registration number. A site with only a contact form looks like a lead broker.

## 9. A clear process

Show the steps from enquiry to switch-on with typical timings. Uncertainty feels like risk.

## 10. Transparent pricing guidance

You may not publish exact prices, but typical ranges for common system sizes show you have nothing to hide.

[[cta]]

## 11. Aftercare and monitoring

Explain what happens after installation: monitoring apps, servicing, who to call. It shows you plan to stick around.

## 12. Finance and grant support

"We handle the grant application for you" removes a major worry and positions you as the expert.

## 13. Insurance and safety

Public liability cover and safety certifications matter especially to commercial clients.

## 14. Awards and press

Industry awards or local news coverage add third-party validation.

## 15. A professional website

The site itself is a trust signal. Fast, modern, error-free and secure (HTTPS) says "this company pays attention". Slow and dated says the opposite. More general principles in [20 website trust signals](/blog/website-trust-signals/).

## Where to place them

| Location | Signals |
| --- | --- |
| Hero / top strip | Review rating, years, installs, key accreditation |
| Mid-page | Warranties, process, case studies |
| Near forms | "No obligation", privacy note, a review |
| Footer | Full address, registrations, all logos |

Repeat the strongest ones. Nobody reads every section.

## Examples

Eco Horizon puts its founding year, grant handling and review rating up front. SunFlow leads with being 100% Canadian and its incentive expertise.

[[work:ecohorizon,sunflow]]

## Frequently asked questions

### Do badges really make a difference?
Yes, when they're recognised and verifiable. A row of obscure logos helps less than one that buyers know.

### What if we're a new company?
Lean on your team's individual experience, manufacturer training, strong warranties and your first customer reviews.

### Should we show prices?
Typical ranges build trust and filter enquiries. Exact quotes follow a survey.

=== solar-incentives-content
title: How to Explain Solar Incentives and Grants on Your Website
desc: How solar installers should present grants, rebates and tax credits on their website: structure, wording, accuracy and how to keep the page up to date.
cat: solar
kw: solar incentives page, solar grants website content, solar rebates explained, solar tax credit content
takeaways: Incentives are one of the most searched solar topics, so a clear guide attracts early-stage buyers | Explain each scheme with who qualifies, how much and how to claim | Show a worked example so the saving feels real | Date the page and review it every quarter because rules change
---
"Are there grants for solar?" is often the first thing a homeowner searches. Incentives can cut the cost of a system dramatically, and they're confusing: different schemes, eligibility rules and deadlines. The installer who explains them clearly earns trust and the enquiry.

## Why you need a dedicated page

- It ranks for high-volume searches like "[country] solar grant"
- It answers the biggest objection (cost) with good news
- It positions you as the helpful expert
- It gives sales teams a link to send

## Structure of a strong incentives page

### 1. A plain-English summary

Start with the headline: "Homeowners in [region] can currently claim up to [amount] towards solar panels." One or two sentences, updated whenever it changes.

### 2. A table of current schemes

| Scheme | Who it's for | Value | How it's paid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| National grant | Homeowners | Up to X | After installation |
| Farm scheme | Farmers | X% of cost | Grant aid |
| Business allowance | Companies | Tax relief | Through accounts |

### 3. A section for each scheme

- What it is
- Who qualifies
- How much you can get
- How and when to apply
- Deadlines or planned changes
- A link to the official source

### 4. A worked example

> A typical 4 kW system costs €8,000. With the grant of €1,800, the homeowner pays €6,200. With annual savings of around €1,000, payback is roughly six years.

Use your own realistic local figures. Worked examples make abstract percentages concrete.

### 5. How you help

"We complete the application for you and include the grant in your quote." This is a genuine selling point.

### 6. FAQs

- Can I combine schemes?
- Do I need to apply before installation?
- What happens if the scheme changes after I sign?
- Are batteries covered?

### 7. Call to action

"Find out what you qualify for" with a short form.

## Accuracy is everything

Incentives change, sometimes sharply. Out-of-date information damages trust and can create legal problems.

- **Link to official sources** for every scheme
- **Show a "last updated" date**
- **Review the page quarterly** and after every budget or policy announcement
- **Avoid guarantees.** Say "currently" and "subject to eligibility"
- **Don't give tax advice.** Suggest customers confirm with an adviser

## Segment by audience

Homeowners, businesses and farmers often have different schemes. Give each its own section or page so nobody wades through irrelevant detail. Eco Horizon, for instance, addresses homes, businesses and farms separately with the relevant grants for each.

[[work:ecohorizon,sunflow]]

[[cta]]

## Use incentives across the site

- A banner or strip on the homepage: "Grants of up to X available"
- A line in the calculator results
- A mention on each service page
- In area pages, where regional schemes apply

## Urgency, honestly

If a scheme is reducing or ending on a known date, say so. Real deadlines motivate action. Never invent urgency; customers check.

## SEO tips

- Target "[scheme name]" and "[region] solar grant" in the title and headings
- Add FAQ schema ([guide](/blog/schema-markup-guide/))
- Keep the same URL year after year and update the content, so the page builds authority
- Link to it from related articles and service pages

## Frequently asked questions

### How often do solar incentives change?
It varies by country, but reviewing your page every quarter is a sensible minimum.

### Should I include incentives in my quotes?
Show the price before and after incentives so customers see both the full cost and their net cost.

### What if incentives end?
Update the page immediately and shift the message to long-term savings and energy independence.
