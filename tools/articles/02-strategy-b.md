=== website-redesign-checklist
title: The Website Redesign Checklist: 30 Steps to Relaunch Without Losing Traffic
desc: A 30-step website redesign checklist covering planning, content, SEO redirects, testing and launch, so your new site keeps its rankings and wins more leads.
cat: strategy
kw: website redesign checklist, website relaunch, redesign website seo, website migration checklist
takeaways: Benchmark your current traffic and rankings before changing anything | Map every old URL to a new one with 301 redirects | Test forms, speed and mobile on real devices before launch | Watch Search Console daily for the first two weeks after relaunch
---
A redesign is the highest-leverage marketing project most small businesses ever run. It's also the easiest way to accidentally erase years of Google rankings in an afternoon. This checklist keeps the upside and removes the risk.

Work through it in order. Each phase builds on the one before.

## Phase 1: Before you touch anything

1. **Write down the goal.** "Look more modern" isn't a goal. "Increase enquiries from 6 to 15 a month" is.
2. **Benchmark current performance.** Record monthly visitors, enquiries, top landing pages and conversion rate from your analytics.
3. **Export your rankings.** In Google Search Console, export the queries and pages report for the last 12 months. These are the pages you must protect.
4. **Crawl the existing site.** Use a free tool like Screaming Frog (up to 500 URLs) to list every URL, title and meta description.
5. **List what works.** Pages that rank, copy that converts, photos customers mention. Keep them.
6. **List what doesn't.** Confusing navigation, dead pages, outdated services.
7. **Back everything up.** Download a full copy of the current site and database.

## Phase 2: Plan the new site

8. **Decide the sitemap.** Which pages stay, merge, split or go. See [what pages a small business website should have](/blog/what-pages-should-a-small-business-website-have/).
9. **Build the redirect map.** A spreadsheet with two columns: old URL, new URL. Every old page needs a destination. This single document protects your SEO.
10. **Keep URLs where you can.** If `/services/boiler-repair/` ranks well, don't rename it for the sake of tidiness.
11. **Write a brief.** One page covering audience, goals, pages, features and examples. Use our [website brief template](/blog/website-brief-template/).
12. **Choose the platform deliberately.** If the old platform was the problem, don't rebuild on it.

## Phase 3: Content and design

13. **Rewrite the homepage headline.** It should say what you do, for whom and where, in one line.
14. **Give every page one job.** One primary call to action per page.
15. **Update proof.** Fresh testimonials, current review counts, recent work.
16. **Keep ranking content.** If a page ranks, improve its copy rather than replacing it wholesale.
17. **Optimise images.** Export in WebP at the size they'll be displayed. Our [image SEO guide](/blog/image-seo/) covers this.
18. **Write unique titles and meta descriptions** for every page. See [title tags and meta descriptions](/blog/title-tags-meta-descriptions/).

[[cta]]

## Phase 4: Build and test

19. **Build on a staging link** that search engines can't index.
20. **Test every form.** Submit each one and confirm the email arrives.
21. **Test on real phones.** At least one iPhone and one Android.
22. **Check speed.** Run PageSpeed Insights on the homepage and a service page. Aim for green on mobile.
23. **Add schema markup.** Organization, LocalBusiness, Service and FAQ where relevant. See our [schema markup guide](/blog/schema-markup-guide/).
24. **Check accessibility basics.** Colour contrast, alt text, keyboard navigation.

## Phase 5: Launch

25. **Implement the 301 redirects** from your redirect map and test a sample of old URLs.
26. **Remove the noindex** from staging settings. Forgetting this is the classic launch-day mistake.
27. **Reconnect analytics and Search Console** and submit the new XML sitemap.
28. **Update your Google Business Profile** and social links if any URLs changed.

## Phase 6: After launch

29. **Monitor Search Console daily for two weeks.** Watch for 404 errors and coverage issues and fix them as they appear.
30. **Compare against your benchmark after 30, 60 and 90 days.** A small dip in the first two weeks is normal. A sustained drop means redirects or content need attention.

## The three mistakes that cause most redesign disasters

**No redirects.** Old URLs return 404 errors, and Google drops them. Rankings that took years vanish in weeks.

**Deleting content that ranks.** A designer trims a 900-word service page to three sentences because it "looks cleaner". The page stops ranking.

**Launching with noindex still on.** The whole site disappears from search until someone notices.

All three are avoidable with the checklist above. We go deeper in [how to redesign without losing SEO](/blog/seo-migration-redesign/).

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does a redesign take?
Most small business redesigns take two to six weeks. See [realistic website timelines](/blog/how-long-does-it-take-to-build-a-website/).

### Should I redesign in stages or all at once?
For most small sites, a single well-planned launch is simpler and safer. Staged rollouts make sense for large sites with hundreds of pages.

### Can you redesign my site without changing the platform?
Often, yes. But if the platform is the reason the site is slow or hard to edit, a rebuild on something better usually costs less over time.

=== what-pages-should-a-small-business-website-have
title: What Pages Should a Small Business Website Have?
desc: The essential pages every small business website needs, what each one should contain, and which extra pages are worth adding for SEO and conversions.
cat: strategy
kw: what pages should a website have, small business website pages, website structure, website sitemap
takeaways: Five core pages cover most small businesses: home, about, services, proof and contact | Give each service its own page so it can rank on Google | Location and FAQ pages add search visibility with little effort | Every page needs one clear next step
---
You can build a successful business website with five pages. You can also build a failing one with fifty. What matters is that every page has a clear purpose and leads the visitor one step closer to getting in touch.

Here's what to include, in priority order.

## The five essentials

### 1. Home

Your homepage has about five seconds to answer three questions: what do you do, who is it for, and what should I do next? It needs a clear headline, one strong image, proof you're trustworthy and a prominent call to action. Everything else is supporting detail. We break it down in [homepage design best practices](/blog/homepage-design-best-practices/).

### 2. About

One of the most-visited pages on any small business site. People want to know who they'd be dealing with. Include your story, your credentials, real photos and what you believe about your work. Then end with a call to action; about pages convert when they ask.

### 3. Services (one page each)

This is where most small sites go wrong. They list eight services on a single page with a sentence each. Google can't rank one page for eight different searches, and customers can't find the detail they need.

Give each core service its own page with:

- Who it's for and the problem it solves
- What's included and how it works
- Pricing or a starting price, if you can share it
- Proof: a testimonial or example specific to that service
- Answers to common questions
- A call to action

### 4. Proof

Testimonials, reviews, case studies or a portfolio. For a photographer or designer the portfolio is the product. For a service business, a page of real reviews with names does the same job. Scatter proof across other pages too; don't hide it all in one place.

### 5. Contact

Make it effortless. A short form, a phone number that's tappable on mobile, your email, your address and hours if you have a location, and a note about how quickly you reply. See [contact forms that convert](/blog/contact-form-best-practices/).

## Pages that earn their place

Once the essentials are solid, these add real value.

### Location or service-area pages

If you serve several towns, a dedicated page for each helps you appear in local searches. They must contain real, local content: thin copy-and-paste pages can hurt more than help. Our guide to [local landing pages that rank](/blog/local-landing-pages/) shows how.

### FAQ

A good FAQ page answers the questions you're tired of answering by email, removes objections and gives search engines and AI assistants clear question-and-answer content to quote. Marked up with FAQ schema, it's one of the easiest SEO wins.

### Pricing

Controversial, but usually worth it. Visitors who can't find any indication of cost often assume they can't afford you, or that you're hiding something. Even "packages start from $X" filters out poor fits and builds trust.

### Blog or guides

Useful articles bring in visitors who aren't ready to buy yet and position you as the expert when they are. It only works if you publish genuinely helpful content. See [does your business need a blog?](/blog/website-blog-for-business/)

### Booking

If customers book appointments, let them do it online. Fewer missed calls, fewer no-shows, and bookings while you sleep.

[[cta]]

## Pages you legally or practically need

- **Privacy policy.** Required in most countries if you collect any personal data, including through a contact form.
- **Terms.** Needed if you sell online.
- **404 page.** A friendly "page not found" with links back to your main pages keeps lost visitors on the site.

## Recommended structures by business type

**Coach or consultant:** Home, About, Work With Me (programmes), Testimonials, Blog, Contact / Book a Call.

**Local service business:** Home, About, a page per service, Service Areas, Reviews, FAQ, Contact / Get a Quote.

**Photographer or creative:** Home, Portfolio (by category), About, Investment, Journal, Contact.

**Clinic or salon:** Home, Services / Treatments, Team, Pricing, Book Online, Reviews, Contact.

## How many pages is too many?

There's no upper limit, only a quality limit. Ten strong pages outperform forty weak ones. Add a page when you have something specific and useful to say that a customer would search for. Remove or merge pages that say nearly the same thing.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a one-page website work?
Yes, for a simple offer or a new business. The trade-off is SEO: one page can only target a handful of search terms. Many businesses start with one page and expand.

### Do I need a separate page for every town I serve?
Only for towns where you want to rank and can write something specific. Five solid location pages beat fifty near-duplicates.

### Where should testimonials go?
Everywhere they're relevant: the homepage, each service page and near every call to action, plus a dedicated page for people who want to read more.

=== how-to-choose-a-web-designer
title: How to Choose a Web Designer: 15 Questions to Ask Before You Hire
desc: Fifteen questions to ask any web designer or agency before hiring, with the answers you want to hear and the red flags to walk away from.
cat: strategy
kw: how to choose a web designer, hire a web designer, web design agency questions, web designer red flags
takeaways: Judge designers on live websites, not mockups or promises | Make sure you'll own the domain, code and content outright | Ask who actually does the work and how you'll communicate | A fixed quote, clear timeline and a preview before paying are strong trust signals
---
Hiring a web designer is hard because you're buying something that doesn't exist yet. Portfolios all look good. Everyone says they do SEO. Prices range from a few hundred dollars to tens of thousands for what sounds like the same thing.

These fifteen questions cut through it. Ask them on the first call and listen carefully to the answers.

## About their work

### 1. Can I see live websites you've built, not just screenshots?

Click through real sites. Test them on your phone. Are they fast? Does the contact form work? Screenshots hide a lot.

### 2. Have you built sites for businesses like mine?

Industry experience means the designer already knows what your customers need to see. Someone who has built ten coaching sites or five solar sites will ask better questions than a generalist.

### 3. Can I speak to a past client?

A confident designer will happily connect you. Ask the client what communication was like and whether the project finished on time.

## About the process

### 4. Who will actually do the work?

At agencies, the person who sells is often not the person who builds. Find out whether your project will be handled by a senior designer, a junior, or outsourced.

### 5. What's your process and timeline?

You want clear phases with dates: discovery, design, build, launch. Vague answers now mean delays later. Compare what you hear with our [realistic website timelines](/blog/how-long-does-it-take-to-build-a-website/).

### 6. What do you need from me?

Good designers tell you up front what content, access and decisions they'll need and when. This is the biggest factor in finishing on time.

### 7. How many rounds of revisions are included?

Two or three is typical. Unlimited sounds generous but usually signals a vague process.

### 8. Will I see the design before I commit?

This is rare, and valuable. A designer who shows you a direction before taking payment is confident in their work. It's why we offer a [free website preview](/free-website-preview/): your homepage, designed and live on a link, before you pay anything.

## About ownership and money

### 9. What exactly is included in the price?

Get a written scope: number of pages, features, copywriting, SEO setup, training. "A website" isn't a scope.

### 10. Is the price fixed?

A fixed price for a fixed scope protects you. If they bill hourly, ask for a cap.

### 11. Who owns the website when it's finished?

The only acceptable answer: you do. The domain should be registered in your name, and you should receive the code, design files and all logins. Read [do you really own your website?](/blog/website-ownership-explained/) before signing anything.

### 12. What are the ongoing costs?

Hosting, maintenance, licences. Ask for all recurring fees in writing and what happens if you stop paying them.

[[cta]]

## About results

### 13. How will the site be optimised for search?

Listen for specifics: page speed, title tags, schema markup, redirects from your old site, Search Console setup. "We do SEO" isn't an answer. Use our [technical SEO checklist](/blog/technical-seo-checklist/) to judge what you're told.

### 14. Can I update the site myself?

You should be able to change text, photos and prices without paying a developer each time. Ask to see the editor.

### 15. What happens after launch?

Is there a support window? Who fixes bugs? What does ongoing help cost? Launch is the start of a website's life, not the end.

## Red flags

Walk away if you hear any of these:

- **"We'll register the domain for you"** in their own account. You may never get it back.
- **Guaranteed #1 rankings.** Nobody controls Google.
- **No contract or written scope.**
- **Full payment up front** with no milestones.
- **Proprietary platforms** you can't leave without rebuilding from scratch.
- **Slow replies before you've paid.** It doesn't get faster afterwards.

## Freelancer, studio or agency?

**Freelancers** are affordable and personal, but one person covers design, code, copy and SEO, and availability can be a risk.

**Agencies** bring teams and process, with prices and timelines to match.

**Small studios** sit in between: senior people doing the work directly, with enough experience to cover strategy, design, build and SEO. For most small businesses this is the best balance. It's how we work at Code Bunny: you talk to the founder, who is also the person building your site.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much should I expect to pay?
Most professional small business sites cost $2,500 to $10,000. See our [full cost breakdown](/blog/how-much-does-a-website-cost-small-business/).

### Is it risky to hire a designer in another country?
Not if you check their work, get a contract and keep ownership of your domain. Many of our clients are in the US, Canada, the UK and Australia; time zones are rarely a problem with clear communication.

### Should I choose the cheapest quote?
Compare scope first. The cheapest quote often leaves out copywriting, SEO or mobile testing, and the redo costs more than doing it properly once.

=== website-ownership-explained
title: Do You Really Own Your Website? Domains, Hosting and Code Explained
desc: Many business owners discover too late that they don't control their own website. Here's what ownership really means and how to check yours in ten minutes.
cat: strategy
kw: website ownership, who owns my website, domain ownership, website hosting explained
takeaways: Your website has four parts to own: the domain, the hosting, the code and the content | The domain is the most important; it must be registered in your name | Website builders and some agencies rent you a site rather than sell it | A ten-minute check today can prevent a very expensive problem later
---
A surprising number of businesses don't own their own website. They find out when they try to switch designers, their agency closes, or an invoice dispute ends with the site going dark.

It's avoidable. Here's what "owning your website" really means and how to check where you stand.

## The four things you need to own

### 1. The domain name

Your domain (`yourbusiness.com`) is your address on the internet. Your website, your email and often your Google rankings all hang off it.

**It must be registered in an account you control**, with your name or company as the registrant and your email as the contact. If your web designer registered it in their account "to make things easier", you're depending on their goodwill and their continued existence.

**Check now:** search your domain at a WHOIS lookup such as [lookup.icann.org](https://lookup.icann.org/). Then make sure you can log in to the registrar it names.

### 2. The hosting

Hosting is the server where your website's files live. You don't need to own a server, but you do need access to the hosting account, or at least the right to take a full copy of your site and move it elsewhere.

### 3. The code and design

When you pay for a custom website, your contract should say you own the finished work: the code, the design files and any custom graphics. Some agencies license it to you instead, meaning you can use it only while you keep paying them.

### 4. The content

Your copy, photos, blog posts and customer data. You should be able to export all of it.

## Who really owns it? Common setups

| Setup | Domain | Site | Can you leave freely? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Custom site, handed over | You | You | Yes |
| WordPress, self-hosted | You | You | Yes |
| Wix / Squarespace | You (if you registered it) | The platform | Content yes, design no |
| "Website as a service" monthly plan | Often the provider | The provider | Usually no |
| Agency proprietary platform | Varies | The agency | No |

Builders aren't a trap; they're just a rental. You can export text and images, but the design and functionality stay on the platform. We compare the options in [custom website vs Wix vs Squarespace](/blog/custom-website-vs-wix-squarespace/).

## The ten-minute ownership check

1. **Domain:** Can you log in to the registrar? Is your name on the registration? Is auto-renew on with a valid card?
2. **Hosting:** Do you have the login, or a written right to a full backup?
3. **Website admin:** Do you have an administrator account, not just an editor role?
4. **Analytics and Search Console:** Are they in a Google account you own? Years of data live there.
5. **Google Business Profile:** Are you listed as the primary owner?
6. **Email:** Is it tied to the same domain account, and do you control it?
7. **Contract:** Does it say you own the finished site?

If any answer is "no" or "not sure", ask your provider this week. It's a simple request when relationships are good and a painful one when they aren't.

## What to ask before you sign with any designer

- Will the domain be registered in my name and my account?
- Will I get full admin access and all logins at launch?
- Do I own the code and design outright once I've paid?
- Can I move the site to another host or developer whenever I like?
- What happens to the site if I stop paying a monthly fee?

At Code Bunny the answer to all of these is yes: clients get the full source code, design files and every account. No lock-in. It's one of the [15 questions we suggest asking any designer](/blog/how-to-choose-a-web-designer/).

[[cta]]

## What to do if you don't control your domain

1. **Ask politely for a transfer.** Most providers will move the domain to your account. It takes a few minutes and an authorisation code.
2. **Check your invoices.** If you paid for the registration, you have a strong claim.
3. **Use the registrar's dispute process** if the provider refuses or has vanished.
4. **Don't let it expire.** An expired domain can be bought by anyone, including people who'll charge thousands to give it back.

## Frequently asked questions

### My designer hosts my site. Is that a problem?
Not by itself. Many good designers offer hosting. It's only a problem if you have no access, no backups and no contractual right to leave.

### Can I move a Wix or Squarespace site to another host?
No. You can export content, but the site must be rebuilt elsewhere. Your domain can move with you.

### Who owns the website if I haven't paid the final invoice?
Usually the designer, until you do. That's normal and fair. Ownership should transfer in full on final payment, and your contract should say so.
