A website brief is a short document that tells a designer what you need and why. It doesn't need to be long or technical. One clear page will get you better quotes, fewer misunderstandings and a faster launch than a dozen back-and-forth emails.

Below is the template we'd love every client to send us. Copy it, answer in plain language, and you're done.

## The template

### 1. About your business

- Business name and what you do, in one or two sentences
- How long you've been running
- Where you operate (town, region, country, or online only)
- What makes you different from competitors

### 2. Your customers

- Who is your ideal customer? (Age, situation, what they're struggling with)
- How do they usually find you today?
- What questions do they ask before buying?
- What would stop them choosing you?

### 3. Goals for the website

Pick the one that matters most and be specific:

- More enquiries or quote requests
- More online bookings
- Online sales
- Looking more credible to win bigger clients
- Ranking on Google for particular searches

Add a number if you can: "We get about five enquiries a month and want fifteen."

### 4. Your current website

- URL, if you have one
- What you like about it
- What frustrates you or your customers
- Who built it and what platform it's on

### 5. Pages and features

- Pages you know you need (see [what pages a small business site should have](/blog/what-pages-should-a-small-business-website-have/))
- Features: booking, payments, quote form, calculator, blog, gallery, members area
- Tools it must connect to: calendar, CRM, email marketing, payment provider

### 6. Look and feel

- Three websites you like and what you like about each
- One you dislike and why
- Existing brand assets: logo, colours, fonts, photography
- Three words you want visitors to feel (for example: calm, expert, friendly)

### 7. Content

- Who will write the copy? You, the designer, or a mix
- What photos and videos do you have?
- Do you have testimonials and reviews ready to use?

### 8. Practicalities

- Budget range
- Ideal launch date and any fixed deadlines
- Who makes the final decisions
- Who'll update the site after launch

## Tips for filling it in

### Start with customers, not colours

The most useful part of any brief is the description of your customers and what they need. Design decisions follow from that. A site for nervous first-time dental patients looks and reads differently from one for corporate buyers.

### Be honest about budget

Sharing a range isn't giving away negotiating power; it's saving everyone's time. A good designer will tell you what's achievable inside it. For typical ranges, see [how much a website costs](/blog/how-much-does-a-website-cost-small-business/).

### Examples beat adjectives

"Clean and modern" means something different to everyone. A link to a site you admire, with a note saying "I like how simple the menu is", is unambiguous.

### Separate must-haves from nice-to-haves

Mark each feature as essential for launch or a possible phase two. It keeps the first version focused and the quote realistic.

## A filled-in example

> **Business:** Rowan & Co, a two-person mortgage broker in Leeds, trading 6 years. // **Customers:** first-time buyers aged 25 to 40 who feel overwhelmed and are comparing three or four brokers. // **Goal:** go from 8 to 20 enquiries a month. // **Current site:** five years old, slow on mobile, no reviews shown. // **Pages:** home, about, first-time buyers, remortgage, self-employed, reviews, contact. // **Features:** mortgage calculator, callback form, Google reviews feed. // **Look:** trustworthy and warm, not corporate. // **Content:** we need help with copy; have 180 Google reviews. // **Budget:** £3,000 to £5,000. // **Launch:** before spring.

That's under 120 words and tells a designer almost everything they need to quote and start.

## What happens after you send it

A good designer will reply with questions, a proposed scope, a fixed price and a timeline. Compare responses using our [15 questions to ask a web designer](/blog/how-to-choose-a-web-designer/).

If you'd rather skip straight to seeing ideas, send us the brief through our [free website preview](/free-website-preview/) form and we'll turn it into a designed homepage on a live link.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a website brief be?
One to two pages. Enough to be clear; short enough that people actually read it.

### What if I don't know what I want?
Fill in the sections about your business, customers and goals, and leave the rest. A good designer can recommend pages and features from there.

### Should I send the same brief to several designers?
Yes. It's the only way to compare quotes fairly, because everyone is pricing the same thing.

Your domain name is on your van, your business card, your email address and every link anyone ever shares. It's worth ten minutes of thought before you click buy.

## The rules that matter

### Keep it short

Aim for under 15 characters if you can. Shorter names are easier to type, remember and fit on a sign.

### Make it easy to say out loud

Try the radio test: say it to a friend and ask them to type it. If they hesitate over spelling, hyphens or numbers, choose something else. Avoid:

- Hyphens (`best-plumber-leeds.com`)
- Numbers (`4` or `four`?)
- Double letters where words meet (`pressservice.com`)
- Creative spellings (`kwikfix.com`)

### Use your business name if you can

If your business is Harbour Dental, `harbourdental.com` is the obvious choice. If it's taken, add your town or a short word: `harbourdentalyork.com` or `visitharbourdental.com`.

### Choose the right extension

- **`.com`** is still the default people type and trust.
- **Country extensions** (`.co.uk`, `.ca`, `.com.au`, `.ie`, `.co.nz`, `.in`) are excellent for businesses serving one country and can help signal local relevance.
- **Newer extensions** (`.studio`, `.photography`, `.coach`) can work for creative brands, but some people still add `.com` out of habit.

If you can afford it, buy both the `.com` and your country version and point one at the other.

## Do keywords in a domain help SEO?

Much less than they used to. Google has said for years that exact-match domains get no special treatment. `cheapplumberleeds.com` won't outrank a well-built site on a brand name.

A light, natural keyword can still help *people* understand what you do: `aristafilms`, `ecohorizon`, `capturedbykatherine`. That's about clarity, not ranking tricks. For what actually moves rankings, see our [local SEO guide](/blog/local-seo-guide/).

## Before you buy

1. **Check trademarks** in your country to avoid a legal headache later.
2. **Check social handles.** Matching names across Instagram, Facebook and LinkedIn look professional.
3. **Search the name.** Make sure it isn't associated with something you wouldn't want to be confused with.
4. **Read it as one word.** Some innocent phrases look unfortunate when run together.
5. **Check its history.** Paste it into the Wayback Machine. A domain previously used for spam can carry baggage.

## Where to register

Use a mainstream registrar: Namecheap, Cloudflare, Porkbun, GoDaddy or Google Workspace's partners. Expect to pay $10 to $20 a year for a `.com`. Watch out for low first-year prices that jump at renewal.

**Register it yourself, in your own account.** Don't let a designer or agency register it in theirs. This is the single most important point in this article; we explain why in [do you really own your website?](/blog/website-ownership-explained/)

## Protect it once you own it

- Turn on **auto-renew** and keep the payment card current
- Enable **two-factor authentication** on the registrar account
- Turn on **domain lock** to prevent unauthorised transfers
- Use **WHOIS privacy** to keep your personal details off public records
- Register for several years if the business is established

[[cta]]

## What if the name you want is taken?

- Add your location: `harbourdentalyork.com`
- Add a verb: `getharbour.com`, `visitharbour.com`, `hello` or `try`
- Add what you do: `harbourdentalcare.com`
- Use your country extension
- Check if it's for sale, but be cautious: premium domains can cost thousands and rarely justify it for a local business

## Domain and email

Once you have the domain, set up professional email on it (`you@yourbusiness.com`) through Google Workspace or Microsoft 365. An address at your own domain looks far more credible than a free Gmail or Hotmail account, and keeps your business email separate from any one website platform.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I change my domain name later?
Yes, but it takes care. Every old URL needs redirecting to the new domain so you keep your rankings. It's much easier to choose well now.

### Should I buy multiple domains?
Buying the main `.com` and your country extension is sensible. Buying dozens of variations is rarely worth it.

### How long should I register for?
At least a year with auto-renew on. Longer terms won't boost SEO but reduce the risk of forgetting.

A website isn't finished at launch. Like a shop, it needs someone to sweep the floor, change the window display and make sure the lock works. The good news: for most small business sites, that's not much work, and you shouldn't be paying a fortune for it.

## What maintenance actually covers

### Security and updates

Any site built on software (WordPress, plugins, e-commerce platforms) needs regular updates to patch security holes. Out-of-date plugins are the most common way small business websites get hacked.

### Backups

A complete copy of your site, taken automatically and stored somewhere other than the web server, so a mistake or attack can be undone in minutes.

### Uptime and performance

Checking the site is online, loads quickly and that nothing has broken after an update.

### Content

Keeping information accurate: hours, prices, team members, services, recent work. An out-of-date site quietly erodes trust.

### Technical health

Fixing broken links, renewing the domain and SSL certificate, and watching Search Console for errors.

## How much maintenance does your site need?

It depends heavily on how the site was built.

| Site type | Maintenance need | Typical monthly cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hand-coded static site | Very low | $0 to $30 |
| Wix / Squarespace | Low (platform handles it) | Included in plan |
| WordPress with plugins | Medium to high | $50 to $150 |
| E-commerce store | High | $100 to $300+ |
| Custom web app | High | Quoted individually |

This is one reason we build many client sites as fast static sites: there's no database to hack and no plugins to update, so upkeep is close to zero.

## What a care plan should include

If you pay someone monthly, you should be getting:

- Software, theme and plugin updates, tested after each one
- Daily or weekly off-site backups
- Uptime and security monitoring
- A set amount of time for small content changes
- A short monthly report
- A guaranteed response time when something breaks

If your "maintenance" fee is really just hosting with a markup, ask for the list above.

## The 30-minute monthly check you can do yourself

1. **Open the site on your phone.** Does everything look right?
2. **Submit your own contact form.** Did the email arrive?
3. **Check Search Console** for new errors. Our [Search Console guide](/blog/google-search-console-guide/) shows where to look.
4. **Run PageSpeed Insights** on your homepage.
5. **Read your homepage** as if you were a customer. Is anything out of date?
6. **Confirm a recent backup exists.**
7. **Check your domain's renewal date.**

Put it in your calendar for the first Monday of each month.

[[audit]]

## Signs your site is being neglected

- Copyright year is out of date
- "Latest news" is from three years ago
- A browser warning says "Not secure"
- The site is noticeably slower than it used to be
- Forms stopped sending emails and nobody noticed
- You find spammy links or pages you didn't create

Any of the last three needs attention today.

## Should you maintain it yourself or pay someone?

**Do it yourself** if you're on a builder or a static site and comfortable editing text and images.

**Pay someone** if you're on WordPress or run a store, if the site generates real revenue, or if you simply don't want to think about it. An hour of your time is usually worth more than the monthly fee.

## When maintenance costs more than it's worth

If you're spending hundreds a month patching an old, slow site, compare that with the cost of a rebuild on a lower-maintenance platform. It often pays for itself within a year. See [12 signs you need a new website](/blog/signs-you-need-a-new-website/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a maintenance plan for a simple site?
Not always. A static or builder-based brochure site mainly needs occasional content updates and a renewed domain.

### What happens if I don't update WordPress?
Sooner or later a known vulnerability gets exploited. Hacked sites can be flagged by Google and removed from search results until cleaned.

### How often should I update my content?
Whenever something changes, and review everything at least twice a year. Adding fresh work, reviews or articles regularly also helps SEO.

## What a website preview is

### You judge real work, not promises

A portfolio shows what a designer did for someone else. A preview shows what they'd do for you. It's the difference between reading a restaurant review and tasting the food.

### You see how they listen

Did they pick up on what makes you different? Did they understand your customers? A preview tells you in thirty seconds whether you've been heard.

### You test the working relationship

How quickly did they reply? Did they ask sensible questions? Did they deliver when they said they would? You learn all of this before any money is involved.

### You can compare with what you have

Put the preview next to your current site. If the difference is obvious, the decision is easy. If it isn't, you've lost nothing.
