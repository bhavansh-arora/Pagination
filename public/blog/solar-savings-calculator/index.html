Should Your Solar Website Have a Savings Calculator?

Why a solar savings calculator is one of the best lead-generation tools for installers, what it should include, and how to make its estimates trustworthy.

Key takeaways
  • A calculator turns a generic pitch into a personal number
  • Ask for as little as possible: location and monthly bill are enough
  • Show a useful estimate first, then offer a detailed quote in exchange for contact details
  • Be transparent about assumptions so the numbers build trust

Every solar visitor arrives with the same question: what would I save? Most websites answer with "book a free consultation to find out". A calculator answers on the spot, and that changes the whole conversation.

Why calculators work

  • Personal. A number for their home beats a national average.
  • Instant. No waiting for a callback.
  • Engaging. Interacting with a tool keeps people on the page.
  • Qualifying. You learn their bill, location and rough system size before you call.
  • Lead capture. "Email me my full savings report" is an easy yes.

What to ask

Keep the inputs minimal:

  1. Postcode or ZIP (for sunlight hours and electricity rates)
  2. Average monthly electricity bill

Optional refinements:

  1. Roof direction or type
  2. Whether they have or want an EV
  3. Interest in a battery

Two inputs get the most completions. Offer the rest as "refine my estimate".

What to show

  • Recommended system size (kW and approximate panel count)
  • Estimated annual savings
  • Estimated payback period
  • Lifetime savings over 25 years
  • CO₂ avoided, translated into something relatable such as trees or car miles
  • Available incentives

Show ranges rather than single figures. "$1,400 to $1,900 a year" is more believable than "$1,652".

The lead capture moment

There are two approaches:

Gate the result: ask for contact details before showing anything. Captures more data but frustrates visitors.

Show first, then offer more: display the estimate, then offer a detailed report, a roof assessment or a firm quote in exchange for details. This builds goodwill and still converts strongly.

We recommend the second.

Make the numbers trustworthy

  • State assumptions: electricity price, export rate, panel output, degradation
  • Link to your data sources
  • Label results as estimates
  • Don't inflate savings; a sales call that disappoints kills the deal
  • Update rates and incentives regularly

How it works under the hood

A basic calculator needs:

  • Average solar irradiance by region
  • Typical electricity tariffs
  • System cost per kW
  • Current incentive amounts

These can sit in a simple data table that your team updates. More advanced versions pull live data from mapping and solar APIs to assess the actual roof.

Design tips

  • Put it high on the homepage and on its own page
  • Use sliders and large tap targets
  • Animate the result so it feels rewarding
  • Keep it fast; no heavy third-party widgets
  • Follow the result with one clear button: "Get my exact quote"

Is it worth the build cost?

A custom calculator typically adds $1,000 to $3,000 to a website project. If it generates even a few extra qualified leads a month, it pays for itself with the first installation.

Alternatives if you're not ready

  • A simple "savings by bill size" table
  • Worked examples for three typical homes
  • A short quiz that ends in a quote request

Frequently asked questions

How accurate do the estimates need to be?

Accurate enough to be honest and consistent with what your surveyor will say. Ranges and clear assumptions protect you.

Can the calculator feed my CRM?

Yes. Submissions can go straight to HubSpot, GoHighLevel, Pipedrive, a spreadsheet or your inbox.

Should I use a third-party calculator widget?

They're quick to add but often slow, generic and branded by someone else. A custom calculator matches your site and keeps the lead data yours.
