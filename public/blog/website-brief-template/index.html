How to Write a Website Brief (With a Free Template)

A simple website brief template you can copy, plus guidance on what to include so designers can quote accurately and build the right site first time.

Key takeaways
  • A one-page brief gets you more accurate quotes and a faster project
  • Describe your customers and goals before you describe pages and colours
  • Share three sites you like and say why
  • You don't need technical language; plain answers are best

A website brief is a short document that tells a designer what you need and why. It doesn't need to be long or technical. One clear page will get you better quotes, fewer misunderstandings and a faster launch than a dozen back-and-forth emails.

Below is the template we'd love every client to send us. Copy it, answer in plain language, and you're done.

The template

1. About your business

  • Business name and what you do, in one or two sentences
  • How long you've been running
  • Where you operate (town, region, country, or online only)
  • What makes you different from competitors

2. Your customers

  • Who is your ideal customer? (Age, situation, what they're struggling with)
  • How do they usually find you today?
  • What questions do they ask before buying?
  • What would stop them choosing you?

3. Goals for the website

Pick the one that matters most and be specific:

  • More enquiries or quote requests
  • More online bookings
  • Online sales
  • Looking more credible to win bigger clients
  • Ranking on Google for particular searches

Add a number if you can: "We get about five enquiries a month and want fifteen."

4. Your current website

  • URL, if you have one
  • What you like about it
  • What frustrates you or your customers
  • Who built it and what platform it's on

5. Pages and features

  • Pages you know you need (see what pages a small business site should have)
  • Features: booking, payments, quote form, calculator, blog, gallery, members area
  • Tools it must connect to: calendar, CRM, email marketing, payment provider

6. Look and feel

  • Three websites you like and what you like about each
  • One you dislike and why
  • Existing brand assets: logo, colours, fonts, photography
  • Three words you want visitors to feel (for example: calm, expert, friendly)

7. Content

  • Who will write the copy? You, the designer, or a mix
  • What photos and videos do you have?
  • Do you have testimonials and reviews ready to use?

8. Practicalities

  • Budget range
  • Ideal launch date and any fixed deadlines
  • Who makes the final decisions
  • Who'll update the site after launch

Tips for filling it in

Start with customers, not colours

The most useful part of any brief is the description of your customers and what they need. Design decisions follow from that. A site for nervous first-time dental patients looks and reads differently from one for corporate buyers.

Be honest about budget

Sharing a range isn't giving away negotiating power; it's saving everyone's time. A good designer will tell you what's achievable inside it. For typical ranges, see how much a website costs.

Examples beat adjectives

"Clean and modern" means something different to everyone. A link to a site you admire, with a note saying "I like how simple the menu is", is unambiguous.

Separate must-haves from nice-to-haves

Mark each feature as essential for launch or a possible phase two. It keeps the first version focused and the quote realistic.

A filled-in example

Business: Rowan & Co, a two-person mortgage broker in Leeds, trading 6 years.

Customers: first-time buyers aged 25 to 40 who feel overwhelmed and are comparing three or four brokers.

Goal: go from 8 to 20 enquiries a month.

Current site: five years old, slow on mobile, no reviews shown.

Pages: home, about, first-time buyers, remortgage, self-employed, reviews, contact.

Features: mortgage calculator, callback form, Google reviews feed.

Look: trustworthy and warm, not corporate.

Content: we need help with copy; have 180 Google reviews.

Budget: £3,000 to £5,000.

Launch: before spring.

That's under 120 words and tells a designer almost everything they need to quote and start.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a website brief be?

One to two pages. Enough to be clear; short enough that people actually read it.

What if I don't know what I want?

Fill in the sections about your business, customers and goals, and leave the rest. A good designer can recommend pages and features from there.

Should I send the same brief to several designers?

Yes. It's the only way to compare quotes fairly, because everyone is pricing the same thing.
