Internal Linking: The Simple SEO Tactic Most Small Sites Ignore

What internal links are, why they matter for SEO and conversions, and a practical method for adding them across a small business website.

Key takeaways
  • Internal links help search engines discover pages and understand which matter most
  • Descriptive anchor text tells Google what the linked page is about
  • Link from high-traffic pages to the pages you want to rank
  • Every important page should be linked from several others

Internal links are links from one page on your site to another. They're free, entirely in your control and take minutes to add. They're also one of the most neglected parts of small business SEO.

Why they matter

Discovery

Search engines find pages by following links. A page with no internal links pointing to it (an orphan) may never be crawled.

Understanding

The words you link with (anchor text) tell Google what the destination page is about.

Authority

Links pass value. Your homepage usually has the most authority; linking from it to key pages shares that strength.

Visitors

Good links guide people to the next relevant thing, keeping them on your site and moving them towards an enquiry.

Types of internal links

  • Navigation: main menu and footer
  • Contextual: links within the body text
  • Breadcrumbs: Home > Services > Boiler Repair
  • Related content: "You might also like"
  • Calls to action: buttons to contact or booking pages

Contextual links inside your content carry the most weight.

How to do it well

1. Use descriptive anchor text

Bad: "Click here to learn more."

Good: "See our guide to local SEO for small businesses."

The reader and Google both know what's behind the link.

2. Link to your most important pages most often

Decide which pages matter most (usually service pages and your contact or booking page) and make sure they receive links from many other pages.

3. Link from strong pages

In Search Console or analytics, find your most visited pages. Add links from them to pages you want to boost.

4. Connect related content

Group pages into topics. A solar installer might have a main "solar panels" page linked to and from pages on batteries, grants, costs and FAQs. This "hub and spoke" structure shows depth on a topic.

5. Add links when you publish

Each time you add a page or post:

  • Link out from it to three to five relevant existing pages
  • Go back to two or three older pages and add links to the new one

6. Keep it natural

Link where it genuinely helps the reader. A few useful links per section is plenty.

A 30-minute internal linking audit

  1. List your five most important pages
  2. For each, search your site (site:yourdomain.com "keyword") to find pages that mention the topic
  3. Add a link from those mentions to the important page
  4. Check that every page in your sitemap is linked from at least one other
  5. Fix any broken internal links

Common mistakes

  • Orphan pages nobody links to
  • Generic anchors: "here", "read more"
  • Same anchor text for different pages, which confuses search engines
  • Too many links in one paragraph
  • Links only in the menu, none in content
  • Broken links after URL changes

Internal links and conversions

Don't only link for SEO. Each article should point to the next step: the relevant service, a case study, a booking page. That's how readers become leads. See how to get more leads from your website.

Site structure tips

  • Keep important pages within three clicks of the homepage
  • Use clear categories
  • Add breadcrumbs, with breadcrumb schema (guide)
  • Put your key pages in the footer

Frequently asked questions

How many internal links should a page have?

There's no fixed number. Include as many as are genuinely useful; most articles have five to fifteen.

Should internal links open in a new tab?

Generally no. Keep visitors in the same tab for internal links.

Does anchor text need to be the exact keyword?

No. Use natural, descriptive variations.
